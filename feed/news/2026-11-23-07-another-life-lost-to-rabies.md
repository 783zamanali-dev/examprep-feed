> Why it matters: This issue highlights critical gaps in public health governance and urban management in Pakistan, relevant to policy-making essays.



Another preventable tragedy has claimed a life in Pakistan - this time in Islamabad, the country's capital and best-managed city. Sufyan Rashid Abbasi, a student at the International Islamic University Islamabad and the only brother of five sisters, died earlier this week after being bitten by a stray dog outside his campus hostel last month. What is even more alarming is that the 22-year-old succumbed despite receiving an initial anti-rabies vaccine at Rawalpindi's Holy Family Hospital following the attack. The young man's death raises troubling questions about both the adequacy of his post-exposure treatment and the university administration's inaction over stray dogs on campus, despite students having reportedly flagged the hazard around the hostels before the attack. Losing a student to a cent per cent preventable disease within an institution meant to provide a safe learning environment is deeply concerning. This is hardly an isolated incident. Far too many lives have been lost across the country - in far too many cities to list. Data compiled by Gallup Pakistan Digital Analytics estimates roughly 300,000 reported dog-bite cases per year nationwide, while the Medical Microbiology and Infectious Diseases Society of Pakistan estimates between 2,000 and 5,000 rabies-related deaths annually. While piecemeal measures, including mass culling, have been taken to address the crisis, far more comprehensive interventions are needed. Authorities should implement sustained trap-neuter-vaccinate-return (TNVR) and animal birth control programmes, along with regular mass vaccination of stray dogs. Equally urgent is safeguarding the efficacy of post-exposure treatment. Given that Sufyan died despite receiving an initial vaccine dose, health regulators must enforce strict quality control and cold-chain monitoring to eliminate substandard or compromised vaccines, while ensuring that public hospitals maintain adequate stocks of both anti-rabies vaccines and Rabies Immunoglobulin for severe bites. Until the relevant authorities treat rabies as the public health emergency it is, preventable tragedies like Sufyan's will continue to repeat themselves.

## Key facts

- ★ Gallup Pakistan Digital Analytics reports approximately 300,000 dog-bite cases annually.
- ★ The Medical Microbiology and Infectious Diseases Society of Pakistan estimates 2,000 to 5,000 rabies deaths per year.
- ★ Recommended strategies include trap-neuter-vaccinate-return (TNVR) and animal birth control programs.

- **Piecemeal** — Characterized by unsystematic partial measures.
- **Efficacy** — The ability to produce a desired result.

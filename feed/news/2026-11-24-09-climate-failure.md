> Why it matters: Climate change is a critical global issue and a recurring essay topic, highlighting international policy failures and economic conflicts.



Global climate diplomacy has reached a point where governments appear more determined to protect their economic interests than to preserve the planet's future. The collapse of COP31 in Antalya, following the rejection of a final draft agreement by the European Union and several other countries, has exposed the widening gulf between international climate pledges and the political will required to honour them. After more than two weeks of negotiations, world leaders could not even agree on acknowledging fossil fuels as the principal drivers of global warming. That such a fundamental scientific reality remains politically contentious speaks volumes about the sincerity of international climate commitments. At the centre of the disagreement was a draft that removed explicit references to transitioning away from fossil fuels while promoting carbon-removal technologies as a means of addressing rising temperatures. Saudi Arabia and other oil-producing countries reportedly resisted language that would threaten their petroleum-dependent economies. Carbon capture undoubtedly has a role in mitigating emissions, but treating an expensive and insufficiently scalable technology as a substitute for reducing fossil fuel consumption risks perpetuating the very practices responsible for the crisis. Equally questionable is the moral position adopted by wealthy industrialised nations. The EU's refusal to endorse an agreement devoid of meaningful fossil fuel commitments may be justified, but its environmental convictions must also be measured against its own energy policies. European governments continue to support fossil fuel investments even as they demand greater ambition from developing economies. Rejecting an inadequate agreement is preferable to endorsing one that legitimises inaction. However, rejection alone cannot constitute climate leadership. Wealthy countries must demonstrate consistency between their domestic energy policies and international demands. Most importantly, climate finance must cease being a bargaining instrument and become a dependable commitment.

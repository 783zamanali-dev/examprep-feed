BY 2035, Muslims will overtake Christians to become the largest religious group in the world, according to the Pew Research Centre’s report The Future of World Religions: Population Growth Projections, 2020-2070. The report also projects that, at the current rate of population growth, by 2070, there will be 3.6 billion Muslims and slightly less than 3bn Christians globally. And Pakistan will have the largest Muslim population.

Presently, the average fertility rate for Muslim women is 3.1 and 2.5 for Christians. At 24, Muslims also have a younger median age than Christians, whose median age is 31.

On the face of it, many would consider overtaking Christianity to become the largest religious group cause for celebration. But what might temper the sentiment is that power and sway in world affairs largely elude the Muslim world. The Muslim population in both South Asia and sub-Saharan Africa is growing. Likewise, the fertility rate for Christians in sub-Saharan Africa is much greater than it is in Europe.

Moreover, the Democratic Republic of Congo is expected to surpass the US as the country with the largest Christian population. At the same time, Pakistan will overtake Indonesia as the largest Muslim country. Both Pakistan and DRC are struggling nations, and the lives of their inhabitants will not be made any better as populations grow.

In Pakistan, high population growth is causing what demographers call the ‘ youth bulge ’, with millions of young people without jobs. Official data in Pakistan shows that last year alone more than 762,000 — mostly young — people left the country to work abroad. This situation is likely to become complicated in the coming years.

While the majority of migrants find work in Arab countries — that have their own labour restrictions — those going to Europe and the US must increasingly contend with social and cultural barriers.

Islamophobia is on the rise in large parts of Europe and the US, where Muslims are treated with suspicion. In a sense, the Pew study raises more questions than it answers. Islam may become the dominant faith, but will Muslims be equal to non-Muslims in terms of human dignity?

Power & sway in world affairs largely elude the Muslim world.

The situation in richer or more educated Muslim countries is quite different. For instance, fertility rates in Iran, Turkiye, Indonesia, Tunisia, and the UAE have dropped to below replacement levels.

But this will pose different problems — similar to those in the West, where ageing populations challenge the state through diminishing human labour and high healthcare and other costs for older people. So, while some countries like Pakistan must figure out what to do with their surplus of young people, others will have to figure out how to handle ageing generations.

Each situation also carries political risks. In Turkiye, falling fertility rates threaten the ethnic balance — with parts of the country having higher fertility rates than others. This is likely to create problems for a country where the Turkish identity tends to subsume other groups numerically, politically, and culturally.

Meanwhile, recent revolts led by the Gen Z cohort in South Asia reveal the peril that restless and frustrated young people pose to regional states. No matter what tactics repressive regimes in South Asia employ to quell the protests, the dissatisfaction felt by young people is real and cannot be ignored.

Pakistan may not have seen the kind of momentous Gen Z movements that led to the collapse of governments in Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, and Nepal. But its youth bul­ge continues to grow, and in the coming years, un­­less the state ta­­k­es steps to satisfy the demands of young people, the threat of revolutionary moveme­nts will only increase — a situation that radical Islamist groups can exploit by tapping into the religious sentiments of angry youth. Keeping that threat in mind, South Asia must work not only to create more employment opportunities for its young but also to promote the tolerance associated with Sufi traditions.

A change in numbers does not always predict a change in global power dynamics. But in this case, the figures do provide justifiable grounds for pointing out the inequity that exists between Muslims and the adherents of other dominant faiths.

Pakistan will soon be the country with the most Muslims. The pitfalls are obvious as resources shrink for a growing population. The silver lining is that, if the state ensures better opportunities for its youth, it will have a greater chance of producing future leaders in world affairs.

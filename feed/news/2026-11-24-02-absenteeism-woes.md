> Why it matters: This article is vital for understanding labor market challenges, productivity concerns, and human resource management issues in Pakistan's economy.



EMPLOYERS face multiple challenges while managing their businesses. Besides setting up the necessary infrastructure, the quality of manpower plays a key role in the success of an enterprise. The top operating companies in Pakistan, the numbers of which have shrunk substantially over the last 30 to 40 years, employed the best technicians and operators, who were much wanted by petroleum giants, such as Safco and Aramco.

Such workers are mostly committed to their company’s interests in complying with safety policies and fulfilling job responsibilities. Regular attendance plays a crucial role in judging an employee’s commitment to his job and organisation. In progressive companies, there is a provision of two weekly rest days, and between 20 to 25 days of annual leave against the statutory requirement of 14 days per annum. The quantum of other leaves in the law comprises 10 days of casual leave, 16 days of sick leave, and around 15 days of festival-related holidays per year. The total number of leaves and holidays is approximately 165 days, leaving just 200 days of work in a year. Despite this time off, if an employee is absent from work without approval, they should be disciplined.

Excessive absenteeism exists in industrial and commercial undertakings across the world. The most common reason that is given is sickness. Illness can be feigned; no doctor can ascertain whether the absence was in any way related to a disease. Employees also miss work because of social, cultural and economic reasons. Many are absent because the work is either unrewarding and hazardous or stressful. An employee’s grave personal issues, too, can have a negative impact on their attendance.

Other key causes of absenteeism include a heavy workload, long hours, and high-pressure tasks coupled with the expense of commuting. Chronic illness, injury, and anxiety make sick leave necessary, as does lack of childcare or eldercare. Workers who feel unappreciated or disconnected from company goals are too discouraged to show up, while bullying and lack of support from supervisors make the workplace unsafe.

Employers should identify the causes of absenteeism.

Chronic absenteeism is the consistent absence of an employee from the workplace. This prevents them from carrying out their duties in a timely and productive fashion, which violates a company’s personal or sick leave policy. The hidden costs of absenteeism include overtime for those who cover for absent employees, temporary hiring costs, and the low morale or reduced productivity of those who have to work harder for someone chronically absent. Meanwhile, when such absentees return to the office, they spend the first few days in either catching up, or attending to pending work.

According to Standing Order 15 of the Industrial and Commercial Employment (Standing Orders) Ordinance, 1968, “habitual absence without leave or absence without leave for more than ten days” constitutes misconduct. An employee guilty of misconduct may be dismissed from employment if the charge against him is proven in the domestic inquiry held to investigate the allegation.

However, employers are often advised to proceed against errant employees gradually. The former should first explore the causes of an employee’s absenteeism and, if possible, try to correct inconvenient factors within their organisations. If such an exercise alleviates the absenteeism of an employee, counselling sessions can then be instituted to address stress and anxiety for all. The result will not only be improved overall attendance but also a content and productive workforce. Introducing incentive schemes for employees who maintain an exemplary attendance record also goes a long way in encou­raging people to be present in the office.

In the case of an employee becoming entangled in a criminal case and imprisoned for an unspecified period, the superior courts maintain that it is unfair to expect an employer to keep a post vacant indefinitely.

In all such cases, the employers had invoked the provisions of Standing Order 12 (3) of the Industrial and Commercial Employment (Standing Orders) Ordi-nance, 1968.

It provides that the services of a workman may be terminated by an order in writing, which shall explicitly state the reason for the action taken and is paid one month’s wages in lieu of notice.

In its judgement on April 26, 2021, the Supreme Court of Pakistan agreed with all the judgments of the lower courts, in which the employers had resorted to the aforementioned Standing Order to terminate the services of employees absent for an indefinite period. The SC also held that the services of an employee may be terminated if he is unable to return to work for a period of two months.

The writer is a human resources consultant at the Aga Khan University Hospital.

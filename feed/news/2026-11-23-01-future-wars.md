> Why it matters: This is essential for understanding the shift in global power dynamics, military technology, and the breakdown of the UN-led international order.



THE four-day military confrontation between India and Pakistan last year and the US-Iran war this year have revealed how today’s wars have become far more complex than the conventional conflicts humanity has experienced over millennia. The days of massed soldiery, mechanised armour, and standardised formations are over. Today’s wars are hybrid and employ every instrument of power a state can muster.

These wars involve cyberattacks, economic coercion, psychological manipulation through electronic and social media, and cultural invasions.

Critically, today’s wars employ lethal technologies, which have left traditional battlefield concepts behind. These autonomous weapons are guided by specialised semiconductors, microchips, and sensors.

The changing nature of war has coincided with the sharp descent of the world order into disarray. Wars and armed conflicts between and within states have broken out in various parts of the world. The pledge that all states had made to the UN Charter to respect each other’s sovereignty and territorial integrity is no longer being honoured.

Two distinguishing features of today’s warfare are advanced missiles and armed drones, which have transformed the nature of war by enabling high-precision, long-range, and rapid response capabilities.

In last year’s India-Pakistan war, Pakistan used missiles to down several Indian aircraft flying well beyond visual range. In the US-Iran war this year, both sides relied mostly on missiles guided by infrared sensors and satellite communication rather than conventional confrontation.

Autonomous weapons lack the capability to understand the context.

Advanced robotic systems are being employed for intelligence, surveillance, and even targeted strikes. Drones and robotic dogs can provide real-time data, improve combat efficiency, and operate for high-risk functions such as clearance of mines or locating captured personnel. Unarmed aerial vehicles provide real-time intelligence, and are equipped with AI-driven capabilities to analyse data. Targets can be independently tracked and even struck by loitering munitions or suicide drones. Transporting supplies and ammunition entails no life losses thanks to unarmed ground vehicles. Drone swarms can now be trained to carry out coordinated attacks. It’s only a matter of time before humanoid robots are deployed for military roles.

The spectrum of modern wars employs other technologies as well, such as quantum computing, which solves complex problems faster than classical computers. It ensures unhackable and secure data transmission during conflict situations. Next-generation structural battery composites can store energy for longer periods.

Future wars will move further away from face-to-face engagement or human-controlled systems. Intelligent autonomous systems will be central to making decisions, often without human intervention. However, this raises some serious ethical questions.

Autonomous weapons lack the capability to understand the context or apply compassion. There is greater likelihood of violating the laws of war and committing war crimes without assigning accountability. Further, the speed and agility of these technologies could cause conflicts to spiral out of control.

These technologies will also be used to break an adversary’s morale by targeting its economic assets or supply chains. During the Iran war, for instance, President Trump threatened to destroy Iran’s power grid and bridges, even its civilisation.

The kind of technologies a nation possesses will now determine the extent of damage that a future war would impose on an adversary’s policy options. The ability of a nation to acquire and em­­ploy modern technologies is thus critical to both wag­-ing future wars and surviving them.

What makes future wars more frequent and unaccountable is the evolving global ecosystem, where use of force has emerged as the primary arbiter of international relations. Major powers have no qualms about using force illegally, unilaterally, and disproportionately to overpower adversaries.

In such an uncertain environment, middle and small powers are looking for ways to fend for themselves and make alliances with like-minded countries. This has nudged countries to focus more on R&D for developing not only war-related technologies but also their own digital and satellite-based communication links. That requires a stronger economy, credible information instruments, and creative narrative-building to deter aggression.

Diplomatic tools must be geared up to avert such complex multidomain future wars. The world needs a new global compact of indivisible security, akin to the UN Charter, to bind states into mutual respect for each other’s sovereignty and territorial integrity. This is the only way to avert the devastating wars of the future.

The writer is Chairman Sanober Institute and former foreign secretary of Pakistan.

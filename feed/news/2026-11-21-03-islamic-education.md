> Why it matters: It provides historical context on the evolution of intellectual traditions, relevant for understanding societal attitudes toward science and philosophy.



WHERE the Judeo-Christian tradition — despite its long coexistence with and exposure to Hellenism — failed at the fusion of religious and pure intellectual streams of thought, Islam sutured the fabric of transcendental and empirical reality so seamlessly that it gushed forth to spread like a prairie fire from Baghdad to India, Transoxiana and further still. The Quran so vigorously fired the imagination of Muslims and implanted philosophic spirit in them that within a short time they mastered the sciences and were at the helm of world affairs.

However, orthodox elements blew this fusion of metaphysical and social reality into shreds and after a few centuries, the real ‘dark ages’ of Muslims set in. The clergy was left with little more than a threadbare formal structure of ideas. How did things come to such a pass?

It was a protracted war between orthodox elements and philosophers, fought from rigid positions which shaped the content of Islamic education and Muslim attitude towards philosophy and positive sciences, of which both Muslim thought and philosophy paid the price. The capture of Bait al-Hikma at Baghdad by the Mu’tazila, with an Abbasid inquisition board, was a shot across the bow to the ulema to make them realise how vital an education system is to state and society. So they set about to organise and control education. Thus, Nizamiyya Baghdad was the first orthodox college of higher learning where Ghazali served as professor. At almost the same time occurred the philosophical movement, which was critical of religion.

Ghazali saved religious thought from the critique of philosophers, but his wholesale rejection of philosophy and positive sciences proved a watershed event for later developments, as his community took his statements as ‘eternal truth’ rather than an overreaction to an extreme historical challenge.

Both philosophers and orthodoxy failed to mediate their rigidity.

However, he failed to appreciate that no structure of ideas can survive without constant interaction with a living and growing stream of positive and scientific thought, and banishment of positive sciences can deal a deadly blow to knowledge as an organic whole.

Ibn Khaldun warned philosophers that the balance (intellect), meant to weigh gold, cannot weigh down mountains and told orthodoxy that ulema severed from Sitz im leben and social realities cannot be the real successors of the Prophet (PBUH). In a nutshell, both philosophers and orthodoxy failed to mediate their rigidity and went off a cliff. So, where philosophers outraged theology by interpreting the Quran through the lens of Hellenistic thought, orthodoxy proscribed philosophy, positive sciences and their instrument, human reason. Free thought, by its very nature, is bound to exceed on certain points, but its remedy is not to stifle it but to keep on criticising it to reach a balanced conclusion.

However, rise of philosophy in Islam was sporadic and individual. It never took the form of a movement or a tradition of enduring roots, as was the case in ancient Greece and the modern West. Confined to theological problems, the Mu’tazila’s rationalism was not purely philosophical as the fulcrum of their thought was to know whether free will was compatible with the idea of a Just God. Muslim philosophers were too enamoured of metaphysical heights to climb down to ethics. Had they made Sharia an object of study, the subsequent history of philosophy and Islamic thought could have been different.

Thus, since orthodoxy’s triumph against philosophy, Ghazali has been leading the fleets of Islamic thought to harbour on theological rather than Archimedean principles. Ibn al-Arabi extended his premises to supplant reason with Sufi intuition. Ibn Taimiyya’s attacks against philosophy are well-known. Al-Shatibi discourages the cultivation of purely in­­tellectual disciplines since they are not related to action and denies the role of reason in formulation of law and moral imperatives, though he himself employs a generous quantum of reason in his constructs. Sheikh Ahmad Sirhindi unreservedly ridicules and rejects positive sciences.

Thus, madressah education, arguably since its very inception, was destined to culminate in intellectual barrenness. The panacea lies in an organic fusion of traditional learning and modern ethos, but given the reforms at Deoband, al-Azhar and Zaituna, the ulema manoeuvred juxtaposition of the two rather than synthesis of the old and the new. The long-quarantined religious knowledge gets fossilised and an unalterable law of God is that fossils do not survive long. As it is stated in the Holy Quran, “…We did not do them injustice; but they were unjust to themselves. …” (11:101). Are Muslims ready to reform Islamic education, or are they waiting for a Luther?

The writer is an academic.

## Key facts

- ★ The Nizamiyya of Baghdad was among the first orthodox colleges established for higher learning.
- ★ Imam Ghazali played a pivotal role in shaping religious thought by critiquing philosophy.
- ★ The Abbasid era witnessed both significant scientific progress and the later rise of inquisitorial boards.
- ★ The separation of religious and empirical reality was a major turning point in Islamic intellectual history.

## Vocabulary

- **Transcendental** — Relating to a spiritual or non-physical realm.
- **Empirical** — Based on observation or experience rather than theory.
- **Protracted** — Lasting for a long time.

IT begins with a fever, a headache and a restlessness that will not settle. Then the patient panics at the sight of water. After days of going from hospital to hospital, the family learns that a dog bite weeks or months earlier, long healed and forgotten, carried rabies. The virus has reached the brain, and there is no cure.

This scene plays out in hospitals across Pakistan every week, yet every such death is preventable. Sindh, where reporting has improved, recorded 285,000 dog bites in 2025 and over 200,000 by September 2026, with at least 41 rabies deaths this year. The Indus Hospital also saw rabies cases from Balochistan, and eight more came through teleconsultation from across the country. Punjab reported more than 1.3 million dog-bite cases between 2021 and March 2026. Estimates put Pakistan’s rabies deaths at 2,000 to 5,000 a year, most of them poor and many of them children.

A study published this year in eBioMedicine, a Lancet journal, which I co-authored with Indus Hospital and Erasmus MC colleagues, shows how widely the virus circulates. We found rabies virus in 64 of 100 severe dog-bite wounds in Karachi, and two-thirds of the patients were children. It covered only severe bites, so it does not mean two in three biting dogs are rabid, but the warning is stark. If this is the picture in a city with the province’s best-equipped bite clinics, it is unlikely to be better elsewhere in Sindh or Pakistan.

Elimination needs action on five fronts. The first is surveillance. Behind every reported case is a grieving family. Much of the recent rise reflects improved surveillance, and the true burden is still hidden. Many families believe the patient is possessed and turn to a shrine, not a hospital; others die in hospital undiagnosed. Surveillance must keep improving, with human rabies and dog bites notifiable at every level, so that reliable data reveals the true burden and where to focus resources. Swabbing bite wounds can also track the virus without catching dogs.

Vaccine and immunoglobulin must be free at every public facility.

The second is awareness. Children, especially boys, often tease street dogs and cannot defend themselves when attacked; peaceful coexistence starts with how we treat community dogs. Every household should know to wash a bite wound with soap and running water for 15 minutes, then seek immediate treatment at an anti-rabies centre, since washing reduces the virus but does not remove it. These messages belong in school curricula, mosques and the media.

The third is correct treatment. Rabies is almost 100 per cent preventable with timely post-exposure prophylaxis, and in our study nearly 96pc of patients developed protective antibodies. Yet roughly half of the rabies deaths reported at our centres occur in people whose treatment was incomplete or wrongly given. Immunoglobulin remains scarce, and too many patients drop out before finishing the course. Vaccine and immunoglobulin must be free at every public facility, and every doctor and nurse treating bites must be trained to WHO guidelines. The Sindh government’s ongoing rabies campaign deserves credit and, if sustained, can save many lives.

The fourth is mass dog vaccination, because treating people alone will never end rabies. When at least 70pc of dogs are vaccinated year after year, the virus stops circulating. In September 2026, WHO validated Bhutan as the first country in WHO’s South-East Asia Region to eliminate dog-transmitted human rabies.

The fifth is effective dog population management. Uncollected garbage feeds street dogs, which is why katchi abadis have the most dogs and the most bites. Views differ on the best methods, but any approach must be hu­­mane, gu­­i­ded by evidence and international standards, sustained over time, and backed by proper waste management and responsible ownership. Local governments, veterinary services and communities must agree on what works locally and monitor results.

None of this can succeed in isolation. Health, livestock, local government and education departments each hold a piece and must work together under the One Health approach. The federal health minister’s in-principle approval of a National Rabies Prevention and Control Framework in July 2026 offers real hope. With timely notification, sustained funding and clear, measurable targets, it could become the turning point Pakistan has long awaited, with the media and civil society holding every institution to account.

The global goal is zero deaths from dog-transmitted rabies by 2030. Bhutan has shown it can be done. A rabies-free Pakistan is within reach if we act together, and act now.

The writer is technical lead, Rabies Prevention, Management & Training Services at the Indus Hospital & Health Network, Pakistan, and part of the National & Provincial Technical Working Advisory Group on Rabies.

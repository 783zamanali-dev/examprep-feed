> Why it matters: This is a critical analysis of current constitutional developments and Pakistan's international legal obligations.



JUST as an important partner in the ruling coalition is starting to question the wisdom of the 26th and 27th Amendments, a report issued by the International Commission of Jurists has detailed the damage the two enactments have wrought on Pakistan’s judiciary.

Titled Judicial Capture: Independence of the Judiciary in Pakistan after the 26th and 27th Constitutional Amendments, the report concludes that systemic constitutional and legislative changes have placed the country’s highest courts under the control of the political and executive branches.

The ICJ comprises judges and lawyers from around the world working to “develop and strengthen national and international justice systems”. The report clarifies: “The ICJ does not take a position on the merits of political disputes between the parties. Its analysis is focused on the institutional framework in which the judiciary operates, and whether it is consistent with Pakistan’s obligations under international law.”

The most worrying flag the report raises is that the ruling parties — in what can only be read as their zeal to have these two amendments enacted — have pushed the judiciary to a state where the country’s highest courts no longer “enjoy the institutional guarantees of independence required by Article 14” of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR) and the UN Basic Principles on the Independence of the Judiciary, among other standards.

It also notes that the loss of judicial independence has stripped victims of human rights abuses of their right to an effective legal remedy under domestic law and Article 2(3) of the ICCPR.

Though the ICJ agrees that prior issues in the Pakistani judiciary, especially its tendency to overreach into parliament’s domain, needed targeted reforms, its secretary-general, Santiago Canton, says the government used those frustrations as a pretext to seize full control of the judiciary rather than enact serious reforms.

The detailed report offers a cold examination of the path taken by the Pakistani judiciary since 2024 and brings into fresh relief what may be the roots of some of the new crises that have gripped the country since then. Its timing couldn’t have been better: there is speculation that another amendment is in the works, and those who partnered with the government on getting earlier legislation enacted are now apprehensive of what a new rewrite will entail.

As local observers have been warning, and as the ICJ’s findings now bear out, the 26th and 27th Amendments have done serious damage to judicial independence. Part of the reason why is the haste with which they were enacted. Before any further changes are made to the Constitution, it would be wise to debate all proposed amendments at length and enact them only after a careful, deliberate consensus has been built.

- ★ The report claims that loss of judicial independence affects the right to an effective legal remedy under Article 2(3) of the ICCPR.
- ★ The ICJ includes judges and lawyers dedicated to strengthening global justice systems.

AS winter arrives, cultural and literary festivals get set to pull together talents from across the country and the world. The first extravaganza of the season was the World Culture Festival by the Arts Council of Pakistan. An impressive 25-day affair, comprising over 1,000 artists from more than 150 countries, it came to a close over the weekend. An entertainment-starved Karachi became the venue for approximately 200 performances. This is quite a feat. Indeed, artistic expression, preferably unrestrained, is a tool more powerful than even social media to bind people, build consensus, forge harmony and strong relations between nations. Pakistan can do with a variety of similar platforms not just to present a united image but also to bridge the internal chasms of class, language and ethnicity.

Undoubtedly, fairs, concerts, fervent debates and conferences of all types are popular means to engage and educate the citizenry. Yet there are not many of them, and fewer still in smaller cities. In fact, the elitist nature of certain events ensures that they operate in silos, separate from the larger public. As the populace writhes in economic pain, the government could do more to provide avenues of relief and cultural interaction. Shared culture requires investment and reduced bureaucratic hurdles for all strata of society to interact with participants from other regions. With the country at an extraordinary crossroads, these platforms become all the more necessary as spaces where people can ponder over ways to achieve a better collective future from varied perspectives. Crucially, common citizens should get the opportunity to brush shoulders with celebrated names and policymakers so they are able to know their views, and to air their own. For this, organisers must ensure inclusive gatherings involving the youth for new ideas, and themes that prioritise the humanities, history, heritage, and literature. Lastly, political discussions, along with favouritism on panels, must take a back seat.

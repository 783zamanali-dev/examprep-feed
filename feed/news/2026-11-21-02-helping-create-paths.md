> Why it matters: It links constitutional obligations to socio-economic development, essential for both Pakistan Affairs and Essay papers.



ONE child, born in an affluent household, goes to one of the best private, high-fee schools, gets a lot of support from home, appears for international school leaving assessments and then makes her way to the best colleges and universities of the country or of the world. When she enters the job market, she not only has her education to bank on, and the confidence that comes with it and the upbringing she got, she has parental and university networks to support her as well.

Another child — and there are millions of children in Pakistan who face these odds — might have struggled to even be in a public or low-fee private school, is likely to drop out well before reaching college, but even if she reaches college, it is likely she will struggle to find a job afterwards. The quality of her education is compromised, she did not have much support from home for her learning, her confidence has not been built up, and she does not have a parental/family network or a university network to support her.

The state is supposed to level the playing field amongst children. Education has shown to be an amazing equaliser and the state, in theory at least, can use educational opportunities to level some of the inequalities coming from homes and families.

It is a legal and constitutional requirement in the case of basic education (Article 25A of the Constitution as well as many international commitments of our state), but it makes functional sense to do that as well: a merit-based society is likely to bring the best talent forward and that, more than anything else, becomes the engine for economic growth, social well-being and societal harmony.

The state of Pakistan has completely failed to deliver the promise of ‘the right to education’. But it has also failed, and quite miserably, to deliver on social outcomes.

Twenty-six million-odd children out of school , poor quality of education for most children enrolled in low-fee private and public schools, high dropout rate, small percentage of enrolled children reaching university level (roughly only 10 per cent), poor quality of university education in most private and public universities and very poor opportunities for employment even if you survive in the system and graduate from it with a degree.

State policies have, in many instances, helped develop & entrench inequalities.

Instead of equalising opportunities and levelling the playing field, state policies have, in many instances, helped develop and entrench inequalities even more. Funding cuts and restrictions for public education, both at school and university level, are a telling example as is the inability and unwillingness of the state to regulate provision of education by private, for-profit providers. Some effort has been made to provide scholarships, on merit, at high school and university level to help students who do well in public examinations, but, compared to the scale of poverty and the escalation in costs of education, all scholarship schemes, together, do not amount to more than a few drops in the ocean.

Private not-for-profit initiatives have also been working in the area, but the scale is a big issue here. They have been and can help hundreds of thousands, but they have not been able to reach millions. If experience from other countries is to be of value, the state has to step in and play its role.

The Citizen’s Foundation (TCF) is probably the largest non-profit provider of schooling in the country. It has been around for more than three decades and has served hundreds of thousands of students and employed thousands of teachers. But even today, TCF has less than 3,000 odd schools and school units that it runs. Pakistan has some 200,000 schools in operation in the country, and we still have 26m children out of school. How many TCFs do we need? It cannot be done without the state!

Equally important is the form of support that is provided. Even when the state has come in it has stepped in to either reduce tuition fees (public schools are free for students and public universities are still subsidised) or provide individual scholarships based on need and/or merit. But a lot more is needed. Again, if you look at examples from other countries young people need support for health and nutrition, academic support (where such support from home is missing), counselling support to ensure better career and occupation choices, scholarships, mentoring support, support for developing non-academic but needed skills in communication, self-presentation, confidence building, and support in making transition to the workplace.

These programmes, sadly, are almost completely missing in Pakistan. Even technical education, skills and training providers focus a lot more on specific skills than on issues of confidence, interpersonal skills, self-presentation and so on.

But we do need these programmes as well. Here are a couple of examples from small/local initiatives. Amal Academy does fellowships with university students to develop their skills in self-presentation, to improve confidence, to ensure better self-organisation and management. This is done over a few months through mentorship programmes that are quite structured and very interactive.

Institute of Lifelong Learning and Development, a new initiative, has a three-pronged strategy. They provide scholarships to ease access, development and mentorship programmes to focus on needed skills and confidence building and a service orientation, right from the start, to ensure benefiting students are ready to pay it forward right from the word go.

The point of these examples is to just emphasise that equalising opportunities or levelling the playing field does not get addressed through access issues (tuition fees and subsidies) only; the state needs to do a lot more. We have plenty of examples we could learn from and bodies like TEVTA and PSDF could benefit from scaling some of these programmes.

It is hard to see how Pakistan could move on the road to better human development and sustained economic growth without the state stepping in and stepping up to address some of these glaring issues of unequal opportunity sets.

The writer is a senior research fellow at the Institute of Development and Economic Alternatives and an associate professor of economics at Lums.

## Key facts

- ★ Article 25A of the Constitution of Pakistan guarantees the right to education.
- ★ Educational inequality is exacerbated by the lack of parental networks and poor-quality public schooling.
- ★ A merit-based education system is considered an engine for economic growth and societal harmony.

## Vocabulary

- **Equaliser** — Something that makes people or situations more equal.
- **Affluent** — Having a great deal of money; wealthy.

> Why it matters: It highlights core governance failures regarding labour rights and wage law enforcement, relevant to Pakistan Affairs and Governance papers.



THE finance ministry’s order asking ministries, departments and public bodies to confirm that they pay workers the minimum wage is welcome, though long overdue. It is troubling that a parliamentary committee had to step in because government organisations had failed to increase workers’ wages four months after the new rates were announced. The committee rightly questioned why senior officials are quick to raise their own salaries and benefits but slow to do the same for low-paid workers. Paying salaries through banks and requiring departments to report whether they follow the rules should make it easier to identify violations. But reports alone are not enough. The government must check salary records, ensure that workers receive any unpaid wages and punish departments that break the rules. Workers should not have to rely on parliament to secure wages they are already entitled to under the law. The government cannot expect private businesses to follow minimum wage laws when its own departments fail to do so. Such failures only encourage private employers to ignore the law.

Yet violations are hardly confined to government offices. Many private employers pay workers less than the legal minimum, particularly those hired on temporary contracts or through contractors. Such arrangements must not become an excuse to deny workers their rightful wages or allow employers to escape responsibility. Labour inspectors must check what workers are actually paid. The provinces, which have different minimum wage rates, must also ensure that employers follow the law. This requires regular inspections, a simple way for workers to report violations, and penalties for employers who refuse to comply. Workers who report underpayment must also be protected from losing their jobs or facing punishment. Without such protection, many will stay silent rather than risk their livelihoods. The law must also protect domestic workers. In affluent households, domestic workers often labour long hours for a pittance, with little power to demand better pay or treatment. Their work takes place behind closed doors, making abuses harder to detect and leaving them with few ways to complain. A minimum wage is meaningful only when even the least visible worker can rely on it.

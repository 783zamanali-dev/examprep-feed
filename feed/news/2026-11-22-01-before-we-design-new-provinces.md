> Why it matters: Essential for Pakistan Affairs and Governance questions concerning federalism, provincial autonomy, and the constitutional amendment process.



THE interior minister’s call for a referendum on creating new provinces, followed by the information minister’s press conference, seemingly puts the cart before the horse.

Consider a citizen told that a new province will bring government closer to their home. What does that mean to them? Will their land dispute be heard more expeditiously or decided more justly? Who will collect their taxes, and how will those revenues be spent? What does this vision of new provinces look and feel like? What will the new administrative arrangements cost, and who will pay? So far, the answers to these questions seem to vary depending on each province.

Without defining what new provinces would actually mean for everyday citizens, a binary question posed nationally can’t provide meaningful insight into the change Pakistanis want. When developers market a new housing scheme, they show prospective buyers what they intend to build. Here, it seems, citizens may be asked to buy into something before knowing what they are buying.

The Constitution already prescribes how provincial boundaries may be altered. Under Articles 238 and 239, a constitutional amendment requires a two-thirds majority of the total membership of each House of Parliament. Article 239(4) further requires any bill altering a province’s limits to secure a two-thirds majority of that provincial assembly’s total membership before presidential assent.

A national majority through referendum may favour a change whose costs fall chiefly on the people of one particular province. This safeguard gives the people most directly affected, through their elected representatives, a decisive voice. It is, in effect, a constitutional veto.

Article 48(6) permits a referendum on a matter of national importance, following approval by a joint sitting of parliament, while Article 48(7) provides for legislation governing its procedure. Yet neither parliamentary approval for a referendum nor its result can substitute for the legislative majorities mandated by Article 239. The question remains: can a referendum, by itself, effect the extensive constitutional changes that creating new provinces would entail?

The case for a new province should begin with the grievances arising from existing arrangements. If citizens travel hours to reach hospitals or government offices, publish the travel times and service gaps. If districts complain of unequal investment, disclose what was allocated and spent. If communities seek greater control over decisions affecting them, identify the powers they require.

Article 140A already obliges provinces to devolve political, administrative and financial responsibility and authority to elected local governments. Proponents must therefore demonstrate what a new province could achieve that meaningful devolution and stronger district administration cannot.

Every proposal should be accompanied by an administrative map and five- and 10-year financial projections. Which districts and divisions would be regrouped? Where would headquarters be located? How many officials would be required, and what would their offices cost? Who would collect provincial revenues, and within which jurisdictions? How would water resources and gas allocations be determined? Do we have answers ready for these vital questions?

The judicial implications deserve equal attention. What high court facilities, district courts and subordinate courts would need to be established or reorganised? What would buildings, judges, staff and operations cost? Where would litigants go, and what would become of pending cases? Surely, access to justice must be among the measures by which any promise of bringing government closer to citizens is judged.

Financial projections must establish what each administration could realistically raise, the federal transfers it would require, and the consequences for National and Provincial Finance Commission arrangements. Water entitlements, mineral and energy revenues, public assets, debts and pension liabilities cannot be left unresolved. Nor can the implications for employment, investment and public services, or the question of who bears the cost of transition, and keeps services running.

An independent commission could examine these matters, publish its evidence and assumptions, and conduct hearings across affected districts. A referendum confined to a proposed province would exclude others whose finances and institutions may be altered. Conversely, a province-wide tally could obscure substantial differences between districts. Hearings and forums would allow communities on both sides of any proposed boundary, including minorities and those distant from seats of government, to share what they want and why.

If the evidence supports creating a new province, a clearly defined proposal should then be placed before the legislatures whose consent the Constitution requires. Elected representatives must vote publicly and remain answerable to their constituents for the boundaries, costs and commitments they approve.

At this critical juncture in our nation’s history, we ought to think before we leap.

The writer is a former attorney general of Pakistan.

## Key facts

- ★ Articles 238 and 239 require a two-thirds majority of the total membership of each house of Parliament for a constitutional amendment.
- ★ Article 239(4) mandates that any bill altering provincial limits must secure a two-thirds majority in that provincial assembly before presidential assent.
- ★ Article 48(6) allows a national referendum on matters of national importance, but it cannot override the provincial assembly veto.

## Vocabulary

- **expeditiously** — done quickly and efficiently
- **assent** — official agreement or sanction

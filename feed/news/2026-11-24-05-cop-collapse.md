THE collapse of climate negotiations at COP31 in Antalya recently is a stark reminder of a world disinclined to cooperate for the common good. The first climate conference to end without a deal since the 2009 Copenhagen Summit, COP31 succumbed to entrenched divisions among the delegates.

The main sticking point was the phasing out of fossil fuels , which was not mentioned in the draft agreement because of pressure from the petro-states including Saudi Arabia and Russia. This omission was rejected by the EU, Britain, and some small, climate-vulnerable states, which rightly hold fossil fuels responsible for global warming. Another stumbling block was the lack of commitment to providing climate finance.

Ironically, many of the countries that opposed the deal for not including the transition away from fossil fuels, refused to move forward on earlier pledges to provide and mobilise funding for poor climate-vulnerable countries. It has been noted, in fact, that many of the latter prefer funds over curbs on fossil fuels at this stage, given their need to rehabilitate lives already impacted by climate change and build infrastructure strong enough to withstand future shocks. This may have been one reason why some vulnerable countries curtailed their objections to the draft deal.

Day by day, the climate question is assuming greater urgency. Indeed, nature has been unrelenting against the backdrop of COP31. While Pakistan was largely spared the onrush of swelling rivers this year, Nepal witnessed a tragedy when a massive glacier collapse led to the flooding of vast tracts of land, killing hundreds. Heatwaves in Europe reportedly killed over 50,000 people last summer, while wildfires in Spain and France forced thousands of families from their homes.

“Every additional fraction of a degree and additional year of warming above 1.5°C raises the risk of irreversible changes […] along with devastating human impacts,” warns the UN’s main environmental agency in its latest Emissions Gap report. The need for cooperation and consensus on tackling climate change has never been greater.

Luckily, the failure of COP31 does not spell the end of climate diplomacy. International regimes built over years to combat a danger that knows no borders are too far advanced to come undone because an agreement was not reached in Antalya. But time is running out.

Given national interests and the nature of geopolitics, absolute consensus may be hard to forge. However, smaller regional initiatives, where countries — especially of the developing world — share green technology and other climate-friendly solutions can be pursued. These countries — which pollute the least but bear the brunt of global warming — have every right to ask for funds from the world’s chief polluters. The question is whether they have the will to safeguard their future through collective action.

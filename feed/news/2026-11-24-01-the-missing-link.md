> Why it matters: This article is crucial for understanding debates on governance, federalism, and the importance of local government in Pakistan.



RULERS who don’t bring power closer to people via strong local bodies want us to believe that they will now bring power closer to people via more provinces. So, the false idea of making many provinces as a cure for our many ills is back with a bang.

Months after it first emerged , the bang is still more noise than a clarion call of reason. I have seen three key reports against it, but none in its favour.

Support — strong on rhetoric but weak on evidence — comes mainly from ministers and friendly analysts. One minister cites Afghanistan’s 34 units. That’s odd as Afghanistan helps us avoid the blushes of being last in South Asia on key governance metrics. It’s a unitary state whose units lack legislative powers and elected bodies. We have 150-plus stronger units (districts) which have elected bodies, at least as per the law.

Some cite unitary France’s 18 units. They too lack legislative powers. Counting them is easier than proving they cause its progress. It has democracy and civilian sway, unlike us. These key parts of the causal chain are not cited in our French politics lessons.

Rulers also ignore the proven merits of local bodies. These offer no instant cure. But with higher democracy and civilian sway present, they bring power closer to people globally. And when the two aren’t in place, nothing usually does that. But they remain our missing link between power and the powerless.

Most of us don’t have elected local bodies, they being absent in Gilgit-Baltistan, Quetta, Punjab and Islamabad and now KP due to emergency rule. Even when and where they exist, they are too weak to fix problems.

Provinces often delay their elections for long but dissolve them early. Elections are often held on non-party basis; local officials often lack a public mandate, as in Punjab, and seats for weak groups are often hogged by elites. Citizens have little say in what they do, little recourse when they fail and little voice in how they spend money. Uneven council sizes and missing tiers weaken them further. Local tasks are split among local bodies and many higher-level agencies. Local heads often lack effective control as vague supervisory powers allow interference from above. Weak coordination and capacity gaps further hamper them. Such split and bureaucratic control is high in Islamabad, Sindh and Punjab.

The setups closest to the people have the least powers.

Provinces first make voters wait for local bodies. They later make local bodies wait for cash. They only spend about five per cent of our total state funds, much lower than in many similar states. They depend heavily on provincial funds and can’t legally and practically collect much cash locally. For example, over 95pc of their funds come from such transfers in Sindh. It is often hard to see how provinces divide money across districts, but easy to see that favoured districts get more cash per head.

So, the setups closest to the people have the least powers. Would rulers who have kept local bodies so weak for so long empower new provinces? Their new capitals may be closer, but still out of reach of the masses.

The aim behind dividing provinces may be to make them powerless like local bodies and actually move power away from the people. What provinces have done to local bodies, the centre may now do to the provinces. So, the masses will have one more government level between them and real power.

Deep divisions within a setup held together tightly by its strong fear of PTI and its weak mandate make the division of provinces dicey. Bilawal Bhutto-Zardari’s diatribes exposing its electoral misdeeds and warning against forcing matters via a referendum emitted shockwaves. Our new troi­­ka — the president, prime minister and interior minister — representing all key parts of the hybrid regime, soon agreed on talks. Perhaps those fond of force have shelved it, for now.

The PPP was largely mum when judges, the opposition and activists faced abuses. It has reacted strongly, now that it’s at the receiving end of the stick. Even though it opposes the idea for the wrong reason — the fear of losing the golden goose of Karachi — the right reasons do exist.

PML-N supports this latest demand of powerful quarters via ministers, but the Sharifs stay mum. Perhaps it wants strong promises of extended patronage before giving top-level support. But it, too, may be forced to speak up when it eventually faces problems.

While our hybrid rulers continue to play self-serving games, a nuclear-armed state of nearly 250 million inches closer to multifaceted crises given their misrule. Uninterrupted democracy and civilian sway are the only way to avoid doom.

## Key facts

- ★ The idea of making many provinces is presented as a 'cure for our many ills' in Pakistan.
- ★ Afghanistan, a unitary state, has 34 units that lack legislative powers and elected bodies.
- ★ France, also a unitary state, has 18 units which lack legislative powers.
- ★ Pakistan has over 150 districts, which legally should have elected local bodies.

> Why it matters: This is crucial for understanding current constitutional debates and the governance challenges surrounding administrative restructuring in Pakistan.



THE issue of administrative restructuring is back on the political agenda. The interior minister has called for a referendum to seek public approval for the creation of new provinces, arguing that the existing system of governance has “collapsed” and needs a fundamental reset.

In recent months, this contentious issue has been discussed at seminars and other public forums, while political leaders have voiced their individual opinions.

The idea of holding a referendum on such a complex issue is apparently being floated to bypass the constitutional process. Coming from the interior minister, the proposal leaves little ambiguity about whose plan it is. Securing parliamentary approval for a referendum may not be difficult in a pliant house. But can a simple “yes” or “no” referendum resolve the intricate and politically sensitive question of creating new administrative units?

There are certainly compelling reasons to restructure the existing provinces to improve governance. But the approach now being pursued is likely to open a Pandora’s box, creating chaos and further aggravating political instability. Several proposals for creating new administrative units may be circulating, but no political party has come forward with a concrete blueprint.

While the PPP has rejected any move to divide Sindh or create new administrative units in the province, the ruling PML-N has yet to adopt an official position, although individual ministers have expressed their views.

There has been an intriguing silence on the issue from Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif and Nawaz Sharif. Not surprisingly, the smaller coalition partners , widely seen as close to the powers that be, have emerged as strong supporters of the restructuring plan, despite its contours remaining unclear.

The question of administrative restructuring cannot be divorced from the broader democratic process. Mr Naqvi, himself a product of a system whose legitimacy remains contested, argues that a referendum is part of democracy. “We all talk about democracy. Parliamentarians represent the people of Pakistan. I request all political parties to consider that most survey reports suggest that new provinces should be created for better governance,” he maintains.

Good governance requires rule of law, constitutional supremacy and accountable institutions.

Indeed, an elected parliament represents the will of the people and, in a democratic system, is the supreme legislative body mandated to initiate such changes. Yet, in this case, the debate on a major constitutional issue has not begun in parliament but has instead been initiated from outside it. Has there been any meaningful debate on this critical matter in the legislature, however contested its legitimacy?

The interior minister’s appeal to political parties to take note of “survey reports” favouring the creation of new provinces is equally questionable. Opinion polls may offer some indication of public sentiment, but they cannot serve as the basis for constitutional change.

Moreover, we know how limited in scope and reach many such surveys are in Pakistan. Has the minister also taken note of the numerous other surveys reflecting public dissatisfaction with the existing political order?

More importantly, the Constitution clearly lays down the procedure for creating new provinces. The proposal for a referendum appears designed to circumvent these constitutional provisions. The Constitution also prescribes a well-defined procedure for its own amendment. There can be no shortcut to such a fundamental change.

The 18th Amendment provides a useful example. It altered almost one-third of the Constitution, but only after nearly 18 months of intensive deliberations by a multiparty parliamentary committee, which eventually forged a consensus. Why cannot the same democratic and parliamentary process be followed for an issue of such national importance, rather than seeking to resolve it through a referendum?

There is much talk about resetting a system of governance that has supposedly collapsed. There can be little disagreement that the country’s political and governance structures are deeply dysfunctional and require fundamental reform if Pakistan is to move forward. It is odd, however, that the rhetoric of resetting the system comes from those who are themselves equally responsible for its present state.

Governance cannot be improved merely by creating new provinces. It requires, above all, a system founded on the rule of law, constitutional supremacy and accountable democratic institutions.

Those now championing administrative restructuring and a reset are also responsible for undermining the democratic process and curtailing judicial independence through the 26th and 27th Amendments , further eroding the Constitution’s fundamental principles.

With democratic freedoms increasingly constrained, the country is moving towards authoritarianism. Creating new provinces or redrawing administrative boundaries will do little to improve governance or rescue a collapsing system in such circumstances.

Phrases such as “resetting the system” and “referendum” are familiar instruments. Military rulers in the past have used similar language to consolidate their power and legitimise authoritarian rule. This time, too, the signs are hardly reassuring. The exercise looks less like a genuine attempt at reform and more like an effort to salvage a failing system through cosmetic adjustments.

There has also been talk of a 28th Constitutional amendment. While the contours of the proposed amendment remain unclear, indications are that it could further tighten political control while incorporating administrative restructuring into a broader package of changes. This raises serious questions about the real objectives behind the proposed reforms.

There is certainly an urgent need to break with the status quo and restore the democratic process. But that is not the kind of reset Mohsin Naqvi and others appear to be advocating. Neither a proposed referendum nor an impending 28th amendment, pursued outside a genuinely democratic process, is likely to bring political stability.

What Pakistan needs is political stability built on constitutional supremacy, democratic freedoms and strong, independent institutions. Administrative restructuring may well be part of the solution, but it must emerge from an open, inclusive and constitutionally mandated democratic process — not be imposed from outside it.

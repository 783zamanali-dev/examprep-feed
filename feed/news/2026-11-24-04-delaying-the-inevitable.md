> Why it matters: This piece offers insights into current political dynamics, inter-party relations, and key constitutional issues in Pakistan.



THE PPP is worried, very worried. Since the talk of the new provinces began, it has been the only party (in government) to take the ‘rumours’ seriously, in public as well as otherwise; the Noonies would rather ignore the matter. It struck back with vehement rejections and heated speeches in Sindh.

And then last week, Bilawal Bhutto-Zardari took the proverbial bull by the horns. At a speech at the Karachi Press Club, not only did he push back against the idea of new provinces, he also created shock and awe by what he said on other issues. He questioned the award of the reserved seats of PTI to other parties; he expressed concern over the state of the judiciary and even alleged the 27th Amendment had been passed by force. He then also called Imran Khan a political prisoner and said both the PTI head and Maulana Fazlur Rehman needed to be wooed rather than pushed away.

The speech did not go down well, it seems. Within minutes, Atta Tarar, the information minister of the federal government that is yet to own the new provinces officially was on television , to criticise BBZ. Others followed suit, though neither the prime minister nor anyone from the Sharif family spoke up. And no one was willing to spare BBZ.

The next day came a number of interviews in which it seemed at times that the PPP chairman was walking back on his earlier harshness. For instance, when asked to explain his comments on the 27th Amendment, BBZ argued that he meant it was passed by brute majority of the government. Then he called Mohsin Naqvi ‘bhai’ after having described him as the minister of everything a day before.

Some feel the PPP, since the meeting with the president, has accepted the inevitable.

But the explanations didn’t prove enough; soon came the news that the prime minister and interior minister met Asif Ali Zardari, the president, and the press release after their interaction said that any future amendments would be passed with consensus as had happened in the past. Released by the presidency, this press release provided a stamp of legitimacy to the 26th and 27th Amendments, and a contradiction of what BBZ had said.

This is not the first time it has appeared to outsiders that BBZ has been put in his place by his father, but the frequency with which this happens is now more a case of the father and son playing good cop/bad cop as have the Sharif brothers for decades. That it ends up making the son seem mercurial doesn’t seem to bother the party.

Since then, the war of words between the government and the PPP has dialled down a wee bit on television, but on social media, it continues unabated. And the culprits are not just the anonymous accounts. A senior cabinet member such as Khawaja Asif has posted provocatively on X, leading to angry reactions from senior PPP leaders.

However, the ceasefire hasn’t put an end to the larger ‘debate’ on new provinces. And neither has it really clarified the position of the parties in power on the matter.

For instance, some feel the PPP, since the meeting with the president, has now, like the N, accepted the inevitable. After all, no one thinks the N in Lahore is too happy with the idea but it realises the futility of pushing back. This is lent credence by the statements of Rana Sanaullah, who continues to insist the proposal has not been discussed by the government. But it is perhaps too simple to assume an easy capitulation despite what the country continues to be ‘told’.

Journalists with sources and contacts in the highest echelons of power continue to inform us that new provinces are as real as climate change. With their access to the hallowed halls of power, they are privy to some information on how the provinces may go forward, despite the resistance from the PPP.

The first step is a referendum. That Atta Tarar defended this idea in his video talk that followed BBZ’s press conference means there is some truth to the matter; since then Rana Sana has also agreed that a referendum might be the way forward.

In addition, a story in The News has also explained the referendum votes will not be counted nationally but provincially. The Constitution also allows parliament to lay down the procedure “for the holding of a referendum and the compiling and the consolidation of the result of a referendum” in Article 48.

Obviously, some Doubting Thomases continue to point out that a referendum cannot override the need for the provincial assemblies to pass a resolution in favour of new provinces with a two-thirds majority. And while it continues to be a hurdle, some experts, such as Malik Ahmed Khan, the Punjab Assembly speaker, are of the view that once the people have spoken, it should be given more weight than the voice of the representatives of the people. Perhaps he knows how this will translate legally and constitutionally, but it does seem to suggest that the hurdles have been or are being worked out.

But this is what one side is telling us at a time when hybridity afflicts us. And the parties’ disquiet is not entirely hidden. The PPP is more vocal but the N remains coy. And till they are on board the hybrid train, they will have a say. In parliament, in creating a consensus and so on.

It seems that at the moment, their instinct might be to stretch the process out by debating the finer points of the question and how it will be conducted. Consider that in a recent speech BBZ emphasised the need to focus on the economy instead of creating new problems. Beyond that, can this issue strike another blow to the hybrid system? Most probably.

The writer is a journalist.

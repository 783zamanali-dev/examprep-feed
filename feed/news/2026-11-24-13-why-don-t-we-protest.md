> Why it matters: This analysis delves into societal behavior, democratic participation, and civil society, which are important for Pakistan Affairs and Governance.



A seemingly minor incident during my daily commute to the office recently set me thinking about a much larger question: Why have we, as a society, become so reluctant to protest? I was travelling in a coaster van that operates on a pre-booking system and charges more than twice the fare of ordinary public transport. On this particular day, all seats in the coach were occupied. However, at one of the stops, a youngster boarded. He was a regular traveller on the same service and must have known that he had been given a pass even after all the seats had been booked and that the back seat had room for only four people. Yet he chose to quietly squeeze himself between the others in the back seat rather than raising his voice against the irregularity. His action disturbed the comfort of everyone seated there. Interestingly, when I protested nobody came to my support. The incident was trivial in itself, but it raised a question that has continued to trouble me. Why did the fifth passenger not protest and why did the others accept an arrangement that was plainly unfair? The question becomes even more relevant when we look at developments across South Asia. In Sri Lanka, Nepal, Bangladesh and India, young people, particularly members of Generation Z, have taken to the streets to express their grievances and demand change. Their protests have, in different ways and with varying outcomes, brought public concerns into the political mainstream. Yet in Pakistan, where everyday life offers no shortage of reasons for discontent, collective resistance often appears limited. Why don't we protest? Or to be more exact, why does only an insignificant portion of our people take to the streets when push comes to shove? Is it a legacy of our colonial past, when obedience to authority was cultivated and dissent discouraged? Have generations of living under hierarchical systems taught us to accept decisions rather than question them? Or is it the result of a political environment in which people often fear the consequences of speaking out? Pakistan's political and institutional landscape has, at times, made dissent a difficult undertaking. Concerns about pressure, restrictions and the personal cost of challenging authority can discourage ordinary citizens from raising their voices. For those without influence, resources or access to powerful circles, even a small act of resistance may appear risky. There is also a deeply rooted tendency in our society to wait for circumstances to change on their own - a tendency that Christina Lamb has phrased as "Waiting for Allah". Many people respond to injustice, delays and disappointments by invoking maslehat - the belief that there may be a larger divine purpose behind events. Faith can provide comfort and strength, but it should not become an excuse for accepting avoidable injustice or abandoning our responsibility to improve our circumstances. Marx's description of faith as the "opium of the people" is often cited in discussions about how belief can be used to discourage questioning. But the issue is not faith itself; it is the use of fatalism to normalise conditions that people have the ability and right to challenge. Spiritual conviction and civic responsibility need not be in conflict. Protest does not necessarily mean confrontation or disorder. It can begin with a simple objection to an unfair practice, a demand for one's lawful rights or the collective insistence that rules be followed. But a society that cannot object to the unfair treatment meted out by a transport operator will find it difficult to challenge much greater injustices.

## Key facts

- ★ The author observed reluctance to protest among public transport users.
- ★ Young people in Sri Lanka, Nepal, Bangladesh, and India actively protest.
- ★ Generation Z in these countries brings public concerns into the political mainstream.

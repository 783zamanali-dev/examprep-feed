> Why it matters: Links foreign policy, federalism and investment, key themes in Pakistan Affairs and Governance.



PAKISTAN’S relationship with competing global powers, and effective participat­i­­­on in international peace efforts, have of­­fe­red the country avenues to exercise inf­­luence beyond its economic weight. It has managed to grab international attention due to its role as a mediator in facilitating dialogue between the United States and Iran.

However, diplomatic recognition does not automatically translate into economic prosperity for a country. Pakistan needs to convert its growing diplomatic relevance into sustainable and durable economic partnerships, which include foreign direct investment, regional connectivity and technology transfer.

Pakistan has increasingly sought to use international platforms to seek its economic interests. Its engagements with multilateral forums such as the United Nations, OIC and SCO provide opportunities to promote trade, attract investment and build partnerships. The economic diplomacy initiatives of the Ministry of Foreign Affairs and its dedicated coordination with the Special Investment Facilitation Council (SIFC) highlight an acknowledgement that economic policy and foreign policy can no longer operate in isolation.

Last month, the SIFC presented an inv­e­­stment pipeline to a Senate committee comprising 38 projects with an anticipated in­­v­estment potential of approximately $40 bil­­lion. This is an encouraging indicator of opportunities being pursued. However, this investment pipeline should not be conside­red the same as realised investment. Inves­­tors assess the legal, regulatory and administrative environment in which their inv­­estments will operate instead of merely relying on the strength of diplomatic assurances.

Constitutional federalism needs to work for economic development.

The problem does not simply lie in burea­ucratic inefficiency; rather it goes deep into the unresolved question of Pakistan’s fede­ral structure. The 18th Amendment stren­g­thened provincial autonomy by transferring responsibilities from the centre to the provinces. Some of the sectors in which SIFC at­­tracts investments including tourism, agriculture, and minerals require provincial participation as they are outside the mandate of the Federal Legislative List. There­fore, an investment proposal initiated at the federal level may still require provincial approvals and administrative permissions.

The SIFC was formulated in 2023 through an amendment in the Board of Investment Ordinance, 2001. However, it is unable to resolve the institutional divide between the centre and provinces on matters related to investment. Section 10E of the Ordinance empowers SIFC to issue recommendations, advice or directions to federal regulatory bodies, public sector entities, authorities and government depart­­ments. This section does not expressly extend this directive authority to provincial governments.

Such hurdles create an uncomfortable situation for international investors. An in­­v­­estor attracted through assurance of a st­reamlined approval process and diplom­atic engagements may encounter delays once the project reaches the provincial level.

The solution does not lie in undermining provincial autonomy or doing expansive interpretation of SIFC’s mandate. Such a solution may give rise to constitutional disputes and alienate provincial governments. The goal should be to make the existing constitutional federalism work for economic development.

There are institutional mechanisms that can bridge the divide. The Interprovincial Coordination Committee provides a mechanism for coordination between the federation and provinces. Its mandate includes promoting a uniform approach to matters of common national concern. Investment-related bottlenecks can be for­mally placed be­­­fore it. The Ministry of Inter-Provincial Coordination must transition from its mostly reactive position to an active facilitator of investment-related coordination if this system is to be successful.

Another option is provided by the Provincial Investment Committees established under Section 15 of the BOI Ordinance. These committees are meant to support the BOI, promote investment within provinces, and ensure ongoing cooperation with it. They may function as SIFC’s provincial counterparts if properly constituted and operationalised.

The Council of Common Interests should continue to be accessible for issues coming under its constitutional purview. It is important, however, to distinguish between ordinary administrative cooperation needed to handle individual investment projects and high-level constitutional and policy disagreements.

Ultimately, Pakistan’s diplomatic outreach must be matched by institutional credibility at home. Pakistan cannot ask international investors to place confidence in its economic future while leaving unresolved the question of how its own government will work together to secure that future.

The writer is a lawyer based in Islamabad.

## Key facts

- ★ The SIFC presented a Senate committee with 38 projects worth about $40 billion in October 2026.
- ★ The SIFC was created in 2023 through an amendment to the Board of Investment Ordinance, 2001.
- ★ Section 10E of the BOI Ordinance lets the SIFC direct federal bodies but does not expressly cover provincial governments.
- ★ Provincial Investment Committees are set up under Section 15 of the BOI Ordinance.
- ★ The 18th Amendment strengthened provincial autonomy by moving responsibilities from the centre to the provinces.

## Vocabulary

- **federalism** — a system where power is shared between central and provincial governments
- **pipeline** — a list of planned projects not yet completed

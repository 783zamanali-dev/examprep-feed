> Why it matters: It highlights the administrative and structural crises facing Pakistan's largest city, which is vital for the Governance paper.



KARACHI’S governance appears to be in a serious paradox. On one hand, one finds political leaders of various ranks cutting ribbons and unveiling plaques around pedestrianised food streets and making promises for more such amenities. On the other hand, a major regional financial institution is expressing its frustration with the alleged inability of the government for slow progress on a major transportation project. The ordinary resident of Karachi is ever critical of the poor service levels in respect to water supply, sewerage, waste management, disruptions in transport corridors, rising road accidents, declining air quality and many other problems.

It is also disappointing to note that in many neighbourhoods, the local government entities have also fared poorly. While the news of ruckus in the municipal halls between rival political factions does reach the common man through media reports, the presence on the ground and engagement of municipal councillors and office-bearers of other ranks is conspicuous by its absence. There are several points that need to be reviewed to bring about real reform.

In the present form, there is fragmentation in roles, responsibilities and budgetary allocations for routine affairs. Take infrastructure development as an example. In the past, this task was largely performed by the Karachi Development Authority (KDA). Bridges, culverts, siphons, flyovers, arterial roads, parks, playgrounds, pedestrian bridges and a range of other components of infrastructure were planned, designed and developed either through departmental undertaking or through consultancy and contractual arrangement. As the authority was manned by capable technocrats and dedicated engineers and staff, the quality of works was satisfactory. Many of these facilities still continue to serve, with minor repairs and adjustments.

A Karachi-level steering committee should be formed comprising the heads of all civic bodies functioning under the Sindh government.

It was assumed that with the expansion of the city, increase in population and other complexities, new entities were needed to perform similar functions. Two new development authorities were created for Lyari and Malir. Similar functions were assigned to them. But the overall performance, quality of human resource, and capability to perform those functions stipulated in their respective laws leaves much to be desired. We now have multiple authorities, entities and corporations for overlapping tasks. We have separate authorities and entities for managing mass transit, waste management, katchi abadis, master planning, water supply and sewerage, and construction of bus rapid transit (BRT). An ordinary resident is directly or indirectly affected by the performance of these entities or lack thereof. Citizens are unsure whom to contact in respect of any specific grievance. Additionally, the three-tier municipal system with a metropolitan corporation, town municipal corporations and union committees adds further to this confusion. If an excavator comes and digs the lane in front of houses without notice, the residents run from pillar to post to find out the real culprit. In an ideal situation, if these entities were responding to their assigned tasks and delivering services according to mandates, the situation may have been acceptable. But this is not what one observes.

The political leadership at the provincial and city level generally speaks in quantitative terms. Number of development schemes announced, ongoing and completed; inauguration ceremonies held; billions of rupees budgeted, processed and spent; and the geographical locations covered are some of the figures of speech in performance claims highlighted by politicians. But none of these helps the ordinary citizen in addressing the chronic issues this metropolis continues to suffer from.

There is an exponential rise in the city’s population due to migration. Poor law and order conditions in many parts of the country and loss of livelihood in several underdeveloped locations push people towards Karachi for survival. The bulk of this population needs basic urban services and low-skill employment opportunities to access a decent living. Urban housing is a perennial issue, especially for those who wish to start a new life.

Due to ultra-high prices of developer-built housing, overcrowding in the existing housing stock results. The extremely low number of large public buses makes a decent commute an uphill task. While BRTs are being planned and developed, they will only account for a fraction of total passenger trips generated daily in the city. Drinking water comes with a cost. Where piped water is available, its quality remains questionable.

Even in katchi abadis, people buy water from shops with unverified brand names and tariffs. There is a serious parking crisis in the southern and eastern commercial zones of the city. Vendors and hawkers along transport corridors are routinely evicted, though they sell merchandise at prices generally conducive to low-income households. But there are no designated spaces for hawkers and vendors’ markets. The city also suffers from absence of animal shelters and asylums. A sizable animal population suffers from cruelty. This must be addressed.

So, what can be done? There are several steps that can be taken almost instantly. A Karachi-level steering committee can be constituted comprising the heads of all the civic bodies functioning under the auspices of the provincial government. This committee must review the individual performance of each authority according to its legal mandate and suggest reforms where required.

The operational and financial details of planned, ongoing and completed projects must be put on a central digital dashboard, accessible to citizens. A complaint redressal system for service delivery agencies must be put in place. Some agencies do have complaint-registration windows, but the general response is very slow. A broad-based consultation must be carried out with the citizens of Karachi whenever any major development initiative or urban planning work is undertaken. Without the people’s input, development has no relevance.

The writer is an academic and researcher based in Karachi.

## Key facts

- ★ Karachi residents frequently report issues with water supply, waste management, transport, and air quality.
- ★ Current municipal governance suffers from fragmented roles and unclear budgetary allocations.
- ★ The Karachi Development Authority historically maintained infrastructure standards through a team of professional technocrats and engineers.
- ★ Local government entities have struggled with political rucksacks and low ground-level engagement with the public.

## Vocabulary

- **Paradox** — A situation that seems contradictory or impossible.
- **Fragmentation** — The process of breaking something into small, separate parts.
- **Technocrats** — Technical experts who are in charge of decision-making.

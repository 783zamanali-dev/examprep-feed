> Why it matters: This touches on the rule of law, the role of the judiciary, and human rights standards in Pakistan's governance.



IT is unseemly that a jailed political leader is forced to beseech the chief justices of the Federal Constitutional Court, the Supreme Court and the Islamabad High Court for medical attention.

In an open letter issued Monday, former prime minister Imran Khan detailed his and his wife’s medical condition, claiming he faces heightened risk of brain haemorrhage while his spouse could lose her eyesight.

“I will only remain able to prove my innocence if I stay alive. I appeal to your Lordships, on the most basic grounds of human compassion: give me justice before it is too late,” reads his letter, penned on his behalf by his lawyer, Salman Safdar.

The government still disputes this, and insists that the two continue to receive adequate medical attention. In fact, Parliamentary Affairs Minister Tariq Fazal Chaudhry, responding to the letter, said Mr Khan is “[…] among the most privileged prisoners in Pakistan,” while telling the PTI to stop “politicising his health”.

But even if government ministers believe the former PM is embellishing the facts, what is the harm in allowing him a medical examination under the supervision of his physicians, as the SC directed in August ?

Some may argue that the many difficulties Mr Khan finds himself in are self-created. There are many in the ruling dispensation who believe that what is being done to him is ‘justice’, considering that he, too, had victimised his opponents during his time in power. Mr Khan’s speeches about depriving his jailed political opponents of various facilities and amenities granted to them have not been forgotten in those circles.

Still, as far as Mr Khan and his wife’s health is concerned, the government must show compassion, if not respect for their rights.

The courts have given the state grounds to deprive Mr Khan and his spouse of their liberty, not their health. If the two prisoners are complaining that their treatment has not been adequate, the government can allow those needs to be met to their satisfaction without making any change to their status as prisoners. There is no conceivable harm in making such an allowance.

Those in power today should also think about the precedent they are setting. Do they want to be treated similarly in the future? The cycle of revenge needs to stop, and compassion alone can break it.

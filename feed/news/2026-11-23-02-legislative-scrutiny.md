> Why it matters: It provides critical insight into the administrative cost, structure, and functional challenges of Pakistan's parliamentary oversight system.



PAKISTAN’S parliament and the provincial assemblies have an elaborate infrastructure of parliamentary oversight and scrutiny of the executive. There are more than 200 parliamentary committees in the six houses, including 35 in the National Assembly, 45 in the Senate, 45 in the provincial assembly of Punjab, 34 in Sindh, 38 in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and 15 in Balochistan.

Each committee comprises 20 to 30 members varying from house to house. These members elect a committee chair in their first meeting; the chair is entitled to two personal staffers, a chauffeur-driven and fully maintained official car and an exclusive office. The committee members and the chair are entitled to travel and daily allowances for the days of the meeting and two days before and after the meeting. All this is paid for from the taxpayers’ pocket and is fully justified in view of the work assigned to these committees. Unlike the British parliamentary committees, which meet only during the plenary sessions of parliament and therefore do not incur any extra travel or daily allowance cost, parliamentary committees in Pakistan mostly meet when the legislature is in recess and hence, extra travel and daily allowances are to be paid separately.

The Indian parliament has adopted another measure and constituted joint committees of the Lok Sabha (equivalent to our National Assembly) and the Rajya Sabha (like Pakistan’s Senate), which not only speeds up scrutiny in the committees, but results in some savings as well. In Pakistan, we have independent and separate committees both for the National Assembly and the Senate. Although the Indian joint committees system is efficient, it deprives parliament of a double layer of scrutiny which is available in Pakistan.

The problem arises when important bills are summarily processed within days or even hours.

In all systems of governance, whether presidential like the US, parliamentary like the UK and Pakistan, the parliaments — whatever name these are known as — rely on the committees to do most of the substantive work, especially the oversight of the executive, which primarily includes scrutiny of legislation. In any work which requires detailed scrutiny like the annual budget, a constitutional amendment, ordinary legislation or, as in the case of the US, confirmation of senior appointees in the executive and judiciary, committees play a pivotal role. It is not practical to do such work in plenary.

Rules of almost all the legislatures in Pakistan provide that each legislation, after it is introduced in the plenary, will be referred to the relevant committee for detailed deliberations and scrutiny. In extraordinary circumstances, the plenary has the authority to suspend the rule and consider the legislation in the plenary without referring it to the committee concerned. One of the criteria by which the quality of democracy and effectiveness of legislative oversight in a country is assessed is the percentage of legislation which is not referred to the committees; the higher the percentage, the less democratic and effective the country and the legislature are considered. An associated criterion is the time taken and the number of meetings held to scrutinise the legislation in a committee. If legislation is referred to a committee to fulfil a formality and the committee returns it to the plenary in a day or two, it reflects very negatively on the democratic credentials of the parliament.

As far as the number of bills passed by the legislatures is concerned, Pakistan has a reasonably good record. In the National Assembly, an average of 50 bills have been passed per year during the past four assemblies. The problem arises when the bills, especially the most important ones like constitutional amendments, are summarily processed within a couple of days, and sometimes within hours. For example, a record 279 bills were passed during the previous 15th National Assembly (2018-2023), which is the highest compared to the previous three assemblies starting from 2002.

Although legislative activity had increased, the way these bills were passed without debate and scrutiny of the committees was widely criticised by legislators, media and political commentators. The coalition government passed 73 bills in the last session alone within 21 days. Almost half the bills (36) were not referred to the standing committees concerned and rushed through the plenary without a meaningful debate.

The current 16th National Assembly has so far passed 162 bills in its 32 months of existence. The National Assembly data shown on its official website does not indicate the statistics about the bills referred to the committees or otherwise. Transparency demands that this particular information should be available to citizens and media. In the absence of such data, time-consuming research needs to be undertaken bill by bill to know the extent to which the standing committees were involved in the legislative process.

According to analysis carried out by Pildat, 39 per cent of the bills passed by the current assembly so far were not referred to the committees and instead, the rules were suspended to pass the bill in the plenary directly. This translates to 48 (38pc) government bills and 15 (42pc) private members’ bills. This, by no means, is a good record.

The situation becomes even more disappointing when we see that such important legislation such as the 26th and 27th Constitutional Amendments — which have had far-reaching impact on state institutions like the judiciary and the armed forces — are passed within a couple of days. The 26th Amendment was introduced in the Senate on Oct 20, 2024 and was passed the same day. The National Assembly passed it the next day and the president gave his assent later in the day. It was notified in the Gazette later in the evening. Such important legislation was wrapped up in two days. The 27th Amendment was also passed with the same hurry.

Laws decide the fate of a nation for a long time. It is about time our legislative leadership gave more time to the committees and the plenary to improve the quality of legislation.

## Key facts

- ★ There are 35 parliamentary committees in the National Assembly and 45 in the Senate.
- ★ Provincial assembly committees count 45 in Punjab, 34 in Sindh, 38 in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, and 15 in Balochistan.
- ★ Committee chairs are provided with two personal staffers and a chauffeur-driven official car.
- ★ Pakistan's committee system differs from the British model by meeting during legislative recess, increasing travel and allowance costs.

## Vocabulary

- **Scrutiny** — Critical observation or examination.
- **Plenary** — A session attended by all members of a legislative body.
- **Recess** — A period when the proceedings of a parliament are temporarily suspended.

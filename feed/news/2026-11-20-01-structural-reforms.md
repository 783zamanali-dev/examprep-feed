THE country’s improving macroeconomic health offers little hope when growth remains too weak to create enough jobs or improve living standards. The State Bank’s latest annual report makes this contradiction difficult to ignore. It argues that structural reforms are essential for putting the economy on a sustainable high-growth path. The figures underline the scale of the problem. The SBP has correctly identified premature deindustrialisation, weak investment, inadequate skills and widespread informal employment as major constraints. These problems have accumulated over decades of inconsistent economic policies. The state’s reform priorities must therefore address the reasons businesses hesitate to invest and expand.

Infrastructure gaps require attention. Tax administration needs simplification, with a broader tax base. Businesses also need predictable regulations and consistent policies. Repeated policy reversals have made long-term planning difficult for domestic and foreign investors alike. The domestic labour market presents an equally serious challenge. While overall unemployment has risen to 7.2pc in 2025 from 5.7pc in 2018, low female workforce participation keeps a substantial share of potential workers outside productive economic activity. High youth unemployment reflects weaknesses in education and vocational training. Universities and technical institutions must develop skills that employers actually need. The agricultural sector needs modern irrigation, better technology and climate-resilient practices. However, the most important lesson from the SBP report concerns policy continuity. Structural reforms cannot succeed through isolated announcements or short-lived initiatives. They require political commitment across successive governments. Every change in administration should not bring another round of economic experimentation. The government must recognise that sustainable growth requires a stronger productive base. Without consistent investment, industrial expansion, better skills and higher productivity, economic stability will remain vulnerable. The real test of reform will be whether it creates productive jobs and raises household incomes.

Published in Dawn, November 19th, 2026

## Key facts

- ★ According to the State Bank of Pakistan, national unemployment increased to 7.2% in 2025 compared to 5.7% in 2018.
- ★ The central bank identified premature deindustrialisation, deficient investment, low skill levels, and widespread informal work as main hurdles to growth.
- ★ The SBP report underscored that policy reversals and lack of continuity across governments severely impede private and foreign investment.

## Vocabulary

- **deindustrialisation** — the decline of industrial capacity or manufacturing activity in an economy
- **resilient** — capable of withstanding shocks or recovering quickly

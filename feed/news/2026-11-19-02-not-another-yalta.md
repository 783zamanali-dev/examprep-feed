> Why it matters: It provides a contemporary perspective on the shifting dynamics of global power and the limits of summit-level diplomacy in a modern geopolitical context.



WHEN the leaders of the USSR, US and UK gathered at a Crimean resort in Feb 1945 for a weeklong trilateral summit, an extensive agenda awaited them. With the Nazis caught in a pincer movement between the rapidly advancing Red Army and its western allies, the European segment of the world war had entered its endgame. The architecture of peace needed to be sketched out.

Historians still argue about whether Franklin Roosevelt and Winston Churchill conceded too much to Joseph Stalin in delineating ‘spheres of influence’, but it was indisputably a momentous occasion. For better or worse, many of the decisions made at Yalta endured for decades, and some still resonate. No comparable outcome can be imagined from this week’s summit in Shenzhen.

It may well turn out to be little more than a grandiose photo-op. A domineering presence on the global stage has additional appeal for Donald Trump following his domestic humiliation in the midterm elections . Vladimir Putin is inevitably keen to be counted among the ‘Big 3’. By many accounts, Xi Jinping is a reluctant host — perhaps partly because there’s only a vague agenda — “safeguarding world peace and promoting strategic stability in major-power relations”, according to Beijing. And he’s naturally wary of Trump’s tendency to grandstand.

The Iran war could figure in discussions at Shenzhen.

The Iran war is expected to figure in the discussions. Some good could possibly come out of that if both Xi and Putin urge the US president to recognise the error of his ways. The conflict is particularly painful for China, given its reliance on Iranian crude.

On the brighter side, China’s oil imports have decreased by design, while exports of its cutting-edge green tech — including solar panels — have substantially increased. Russia, on the other hand, has been delighted by a sanctions reprieve and a surge in petroleum exports, largely due to the transport hiatus in the Hormuz and Bab al-Mandab straits. Besides, the US/Israeli conflict helps Russia portray its war against Ukraine as a comparable venture aimed at safeguarding its security. Moscow’s stance makes slightly more sense than the justifications from Washington and Tel Aviv for their aggression against Iran. China sees both wars as huge strategic errors.

Trump seems to believe the Israeli propaganda that Tehran must be prevented from acquiring a nuclear weapon, an outcome that now logically seems likelier than it did before the attacks. That may help to explain why nuclear proliferation was among the topics he hoped to broach at his talks with Xi and Putin. Back in February, his administration turned down Moscow’s proposal to extend a strategic arms reduction treaty, with Trump aspiring for a new deal that includes China, whose expanding nuclear arsenal appears to have alarmed Sinophobes in the US. Given that even its projected stockpiles are a fraction of what’s stored in Russian and American silos, Beijing does not wish to play ball.

Shortly before flying to Asia, Trump noted that denuclearisation, Iran, economics, trade and “my favourite topic, Ukraine” would be up for discussion. Volodymyr Zelensky is alarmed by the possibilities, but a carve-up without Kyiv at the table is hardly likely. China sees Ukraine as a European issue that should be discussed at a more appropriate forum. Trump is disinclined, meanwhile, to bring up the Nvidia fiasco after the US chip-maker’s CEO Jensen Huang was ousted over revelations about an affair with a purported Chinese ‘asset’.

America rem­ains determined to deny China superiority in artificial intelligence throu­­gh curbs on chip exports. But that’s probably another lost battle. Exactly what the three lea­ders will talk about remains to be seen.

Climate change won’t be on the agenda. That’s being thrashed out at COP31 in Antalya, Türkiye, where there’s no official US representation, given the Trump administration’s focus on exacerbating global heating despite rapidly mounting evidence about its catastrophic consequences, from the devastation in Nepal caused by glacier collapse to an estimated 50,000 heatwave deaths in Europe during a scorching summer, not to mention the ecocidal aspect of the Pales­tinian genocide as well as the Iranian, Ukrainian and Sudanese conflicts. Not to mention the ginormous carbon footprint of proliferating US data centres.

As Swedish activist Greta Thunberg accurately spelt it out in Antalya, “The planet is not dying, it is being killed.” And the outgoing UN Secretary-General, Antonio Guterres, was equally correct in pointing out: “It’s make or bake time.” He might have erred, though, in comparing fossil fuels to dinosaurs and claiming the latter “did not have a lobby”. They do, and some of its components might be congregating in Shenzhen right now.

## Vocabulary

- **Grandstand** — To act in a way to impress onlookers.
- **Pincer movement** — A military tactic where an enemy is attacked simultaneously from two sides.
- **Trilateral** — Involving three parties.

> Why it matters: This provides context on regional diplomacy and potential future pathways for India-Pakistan relations.



THAT Pakistan is sending a minister to attend the SCO Civilisational Dialogue in New Delhi on Nov 20 should be applauded even if it’s not clear that this could be the proverbial ice breaker between the perpetually aloof neighbours. It is, however, worth hoping for.

The agenda for a civilisational dialogue should be fun for the participants, although India’s decade-old Hindu revivalist government may drag its feet on the issue. Bilateral ties can still improve despite this handicap. Reports are circulating of cross-border trade also picking up — albeit discreetly — between India and Pakistan in recent days.

Renowned political analyst Trita Parsi used a phrase to describe the options for a low-key US retreat from the war-torn Gulf region, which may be relevant for India-Pakistan ties. Parsi likened Donald Trump’s options for a face-saving pullout from his self-created mess to Swiss cheese. Sanctions on paper and holes inside, like the bubbles within a good Swiss cheese. The US could keep its formal sanctions on the books so the administration can publicly claim it is still maintaining pressure on Tehran. In reality, the enforcement is invisibly relaxed. Which is what might work, if nothing else does, for the South Asian neighbours — an unannounced, even surreptitious discarding of different stages of hostilities.

Behind the scenes, in Parsi’s version of a workable Gulf resolution, the US gradually loosens its naval blockade and stops aggressively enforcing sanctions. This creates large “holes” in the economic pressure — much like the Swiss cheese — allowing Asian, Middle Eastern, and Gulf countries to resume trading with and investing in Iran without fear of US retaliation. At some opportune moment, even if the currently tense atmosphere results in another catastrophic flare-up, peace would be the only game in town, possibly giving credence to Trita Parsi’s face-saving device.

The immediate trigger for the latest round of signalling, if it indeed adds up to some kind of undeclared rapprochement, springs from very practical and commonsensical exigencies that require all neighbours to collectively deal with the huge economic challenges dogging the world. The platform offered by the SCO has been used and abused by both sides in the past to launch verbal attacks on each other. Could the tide have turned?

The agenda at hand for Dr Musadik Malik, Federal Minister for Climate Change and Environmental Coordination, representing Pak­is­tan at the meeting could present challenges as well as chances to resolve them. However, the lar­ger theme of a civilisational dialogue that brings all SCO members together may have to confront some predictable handwringing from India.

An unannounced, even surreptitious discarding of different stages of hostilities could work for South Asia.

The idea of civilisation imagines a palpable and unbroken link between the past and the present. In its understanding of India’s past, however, the Hindutva establishment in New Delhi has manufactured demons to comply with a narrow political agenda. Its search for a golden past requires changing names of streets, and identifying, abusing and occasionally lynching the conjured enemy.

Pakistan has been through this motion in the past. That’s when schoolchildren were given controversial ideas about non-Muslims. An instructive moment came when a Muslim journalist from India who accompanied Atal Bihari Vajpayee on his visit to Pakistan in 1978 went with his colleagues to meet his cousins. At the emotional meeting, they also wanted to know discreetly if K.K. Katyal, Raghu Rai and Sumit Chakravarty were Hindu. “But they are just like us,” one cousin reportedly observed after being told of their Hindu identities.

Today many centres in Pakistan are exploring and retrieving old names of their neighbourhoods with a view to restoring their Hindu or other non-Muslim identity. India, with the advent of Narendra Modi, is heading in the opposite direction. Mughal rule is being airbrushed from school curriculum.

If more Indians could travel to Pakistan they would be able to see things similar to what I saw. A few days before the Lahore summit, I travelled with Sushma Swaraj and K.R. Malkani to the fabled Katas Raj Temple abutting the Lahore-Islamabad motorway. Malkani, an atheist, stood there looking amused as Swaraj dipped her hands into the sacred pond thought to have been used by the Pandavas during their exile. The caring way this temple and countless others have been preserved for Indian tourists will make more sense when people travel across the borders.

This is not to say that minorities don’t face prejudice in Pakistan. Pakistani human rights activists have spoken strongly against forcible marriage of Hindu girls. The government has made some laws to deter the phenomenon but its impact is not uniformly felt.

The prevalent bigotry on the Indian side finds state support often enough as it assumes the form of homes, mosques and churches being vandalised or bulldozed accompanied by the occasional lynching of individuals from minorities. There seems to be a competition between bigots from both sides.

At the SCO dialogue, a two-day session will centre on civilisational and cultural expression, art and architecture, language and literature, and religious and philosophical traditions. Delegates will also deliberate on filing joint multinational nominations for Unesco World Heritage recognition based on centuries of shared cultural exchange. Much of this will be problematic for the current ruling dispensation in India.

In 2015, it had dubiously rejected a Unesco plan to declare Delhi a World Heritage City encompassing Old Delhi with its Mughal-era icons and British-era Lutyens’ New Delhi.

To conclude, a telling story from the Maldives where Muslim zealots set fire to a Pakistani pavilion during a 2011 Saarc event and India watched the mayhem without a word of camaraderie with Pakistan. The event involved a display of national cultural treasures by Saarc states. Pakistan showcased its Buddhist heritage with tableaux of relics from Moenjodaro’s Indus civilisation. Extremists set fire to the Pakistan pavilion. The SCO dialogue offers as good an occasion as any to make amends to this witless aloofness between the two.

The writer is Dawn’s correspondent in Delhi.

> Why it matters: This historical account provides valuable context on colonial economic practices, resource exploitation, and the origins of major energy companies, relevant to Pakistan Affairs and economic history.



Along the banks of the Irrawaddy, in the dusty plains of Yenangyaung, the people of Burma had been extracting oil from the earth for centuries. The wells were hand-dug, narrow and deep, and belonged to hereditary families. The crude was used in lamps, while a coating of oil protected wooden boats against water and decay. Few could have foreseen that this modest local trade would eventually become an important part of the petroleum industry of the British Empire. In 1886, a year after the British annexed Upper Burma and sent King Thibaw into exile, a Scottish merchant named David Sime Cargill founded the Burmah Oil Company in Glasgow. Mechanised operations gradually replaced traditional methods, a refinery was established at Syriam near Rangoon, and kerosene began finding markets across the subcontinent. Burmah Oil grew into one of Asia's major oil companies. Its interests extended well beyond Burma. The company financed William Knox D'Arcy's search for oil in Persia, an undertaking that led to the establishment of the Anglo-Persian Oil Company, later known as BP. In India, Burmah Oil took over the Assam Oil Company in stages, eventually operating through it the historic Digboi oilfield. It also joined hands with Shell in a petroleum marketing venture known as Burmah-Shell. The company's influence extended to Pakistan as well, leaving a legacy that persists today. By early 1942, Japanese forces had overrun Malaya and Singapore and were advancing into Burma. The British authorities were determined not to allow the Japanese war machine to take possession of the country's oil resources. Burmah Oil's engineers were instructed to demolish the very facilities they had spent years developing. Wells were plugged, pumps disabled, storage tanks destroyed and refineries demolished, extensively damaging the oilfields of Yenangyaung and Chauk. When the Japanese occupied the area, the infrastructure had been rendered unusable, frustrating their war effort. Burmah Oil had complied with the British government's wartime requirements, but the question of compensation proved far more contentious. After the war, the company and associated claimants sought compensation exceeding &pound;31 million for property destroyed at the government's direction. The postwar response was inadequate: the British government provided approximately &pound;4.75 million from a wider allocation of &pound;10 million for rehabilitation, but refused to meet the balance of the claims. Maintaining that it was entitled to compensation under the law, the company took the Crown to court. Since the company was headquartered in Glasgow, proceedings were brought in Scotland against the Lord Advocate. A Scottish judge initially ruled in the claimants' favour, but the senior judges of the Court of Session in Edinburgh reversed the decision. Burmah Oil then appealed to the House of Lords. For readers in the subcontinent, it is worth explaining the unusual position occupied by the House of Lords in the British constitutional system. It was the upper chamber of Parliament, comprising members mostly without legal qualifications, but until 2009 it also served as Britain's highest court of appeal through a small group of senior judges known as the Law Lords. On 21 April 1964, the Law Lords decided the Burmah Oil case by a majority of three to two, holding that the destruction of the company's installations, although lawful as a wartime measure, gave rise to a right to compensation. The company had secured a significant legal victory before Britain's highest court. The victory was short-lived. Harold Wilson's government was alarmed by the possible financial consequences and substantial Treasury liabilities if other claimants sought similar payments. There was no higher court to appeal to, so Parliament intervened. In 1965, Parliament passed the War Damage Act, retrospectively abolishing the common-law right to compensation for property damaged or destroyed by lawful acts of the Crown during wartime, applying it even to proceedings already under way. The legislation removed the legal foundation of Burmah Oil's claim, preventing the company from securing the practical benefit of its victory. The episode remains a striking example of the limits of judicial protection against retrospective legislation when government finances are threatened. Burmah Oil's financial fortunes subsequently took a dramatic turn. By the mid-1970s, heavy losses in its tanker business and an American oil acquisition - financed partly through borrowing against its BP shareholding - brought the company close to collapse amidst falling tanker rates and a sharp decline in BP's share price. The Bank of England intervened in January 1975 by purchasing Burmah Oil's BP shares to prevent a disorderly failure. Burmah subsequently reorganised its business around Castrol, the lubricants company acquired in 1966, becoming Burmah-Castrol before being acquired in 2000 by BP Amoco. Its Pakistan interests had passed into Pakistan Petroleum Limited (PPL), established in 1950 with Burmah Oil as its major shareholder. PPL went on to discover the vast Sui gas field in 1952, an event of lasting importance to Pakistan's energy sector. The history of Burmah Oil deserves to be remembered for more than its contribution to the petroleum industry of Burma, India and Pakistan. It built an extensive business, destroyed its own installations at the British government's direction during the Second World War, and spent years pursuing compensation through the courts. Even after obtaining a favourable judgment from Britain's highest court, it found that Parliament could deprive it of the practical benefit of its victory. The company was expected to make the sacrifice when the government required it, but was left to bear much of the loss when the war was over. It is a reminder that the interests of the state and the rights of those who serve its purposes do not always coincide.

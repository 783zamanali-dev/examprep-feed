WORLD Children’s Day, observed today, should be a celebration of childhood. In Pakistan, it is a reminder of how often children are denied even basic safety. At least 28 schoolchildren died when a roof collapsed in Peshawar last month; in August, a fire at Islamabad’s Pims hospital killed 14 newborns. Both tragedies raise serious questions about safety standards in schools and hospitals. Reports of children raped and murdered, sometimes by people they knew, point to dangers closer to home. Sahil documented 1,914 child abuse cases in the first half of 2026, including 1,015 cases of sexual abuse and 558 abductions. These were only cases reported in newspapers; the true scale of abuse is likely far greater. Children also face exploitation online. A Unicef-backed study estimated that 1.5m internet-using children aged 12 to 17 experienced technology-facilitated sexual exploitation or abuse in a year. Yet despite the scale of these dangers, effective protection remains out of reach.

Violence and abuse are only part of the hardship confronting Pakistan’s children. Unicef estimates that 25.1m children aged five to 16 are out of school. Malnutrition and preventable disease remain widespread, while nine polio cases had been reported by early November. Child marriage continues to interrupt girls’ education and expose them to early motherhood. There has been some progress. Punjab joined Sindh, Balochistan and Islamabad this year in setting 18 as the minimum marriage age, though KP has yet to follow suit. The Benazir Nashonuma Programme has also been extended to support more vulnerable women and children. But laws need enforcement, and schemes need sustained funding and oversight, particularly in areas where public services are weakest. School and hospital safety inspections, effective investigations of abuse, support for survivors and stronger safeguards online are essential. Birth registration, vaccination, nutrition and access to schooling also demand attention. Officials must be held accountable for disasters, and investigations must lead to action. Pakistan cannot keep failing its children and then celebrating them once a year. The real disgrace is not that these failures are unknown, but that they are allowed to continue.

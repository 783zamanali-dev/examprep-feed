> Why it matters: This piece is essential for comprehending the historical evolution of Indian politics, the rise of religious nationalism, and its implications for regional dynamics.



TERRIFIED by Rahul and Priyanka’s exposure to lurking harm as the siblings negotiate the remorseless world of Indian politics, Sonia Gandhi asks herself sagely in her memoir: “Is it because I understand even more clearly and only too well what is at stake, and the extent to which our adversary is ruthless and unprincipled? Our opponents have always used the garb of religion to further a regressive, right-wing agenda — only now, as their ideology has gained traction, they have become more hardened and violent.”

In describing the right wing’s propensity for violence as a dominant threat to democracy (and to her children), Gandhi reveals an ideological connect with Jawaharlal Nehru. It was Nehru who, soon after independence, would tell his advisers to worry about Hindu extremists rather than communists (who the advisers preferred to target) as the potential destroyers of his idea of secular democracy.

Nehru’s ideas were carried forward by Indira Gandhi in her 1966-77 tenure. She was supported in the task by moderate communists. But she soiled her progressive credentials with the 1975 emergency.

The second tenure in 1980 saw her losing the left’s support and it became an uphill toil for Indira to preserve Nehru’s secular focus while Hindutva, emboldened by its camouflaged 1977 entry into mainstream politics, chipped away at the pillars of secular India, hollowing them out eventually. Rajiv Gandhi saw religion as the flavour of his time. Thus, a man not known for any regressive proclivities plunged headlong into the religious mire he would unwittingly deepen. By siding with Muslim reactionaries in the Shahbano alimony case, he found himself needing to balance the huge political error by appeasing right-wing Hindus in the Babri Masjid dispute. Clearly his advisers were up to no good and several of them proved it by crossing over to the BJP.

Rahul Gandhi can be vividly stern with the right wing at home while favouring a gentler diplomatic vocabulary for outrages abroad.

The ideological focus was blurred further by Manmohan Singh, who put Nehru’s worldview in the loft and asserted instead that the biggest internal threat to the country came from the hard-line left, not, as Nehru had warned, from the Hindu right. Singh was regurgitating the traditionally anti-left propaganda of big businesses. They backed his vertical ascent and used the time to prepare the grounds for Narendra Modi’s arrival. By the time Singh woke up and described Modi as a disaster for India, it was too late. His secular legacy was to have produced the Justice Sachar report about the miserable state Muslims live in, which nobody today wants to talk about.

As for Rahul Gandhi, for reasons best known to him, his fulminations in the US last week detracted from his mother’s calling out the mass murder in Gaza as a genocide that India needed to oppose as its moral tenet. Sonia Gandhi stood out as a rare Indian leader to champion Palestinian rights in Modi’s India. She slammed the government in an opinion piece in a major daily for humouring Israel with its loud diplomatic silence on Gaza. Like her mother, Priyanka too described the genocide as genocide. Rahul hesitantly uses the word to describe Gaza’s nightmare but he is not averse to calling it out as genocide.

He offered a tame response instead to students in New York, reportedly saying one wrong could not be justified by another wrong, the reference being to Hamas as the trigger. In a world polarised by Israeli atrocities, Rahul thus embroiled himself in the notorious action-reaction trope used by right-wing groups mostly to justify targeting weaker quarries.

It is of course possible that being leader of the opposition in the Lok Sabha requires Rahul to be less strident on the diplomatic circuit than his mother or sister are able to be. There’s another likelier reason to be circumspect — the need to keep the US close. It has been suggested that since India’s diplomatic ties with Israel were established in January 1992 within months of Rajiv Gandhi’s assassination, he was somehow the impediment. What is less known is that while Rajiv maintained a formal non-alignment and pro-Palestinian posture, he did take incremental steps towards engagement with Israel. A significant example was that an Israeli tennis team was allowed to tour India in 1987.

Rajiv also yielded to American Jewish leaders ostensibly to get more traction for India in Washington. It resulted in the elevation of the Israeli vice-consul in Bombay to the rank of consul, followed by indirect contacts in Washington and New York in 1988. But despite the limited outreach, he continued to publicly criticise Israeli policies — such as condemning the 1985 attack on PLO offices in Tunis — and maintained support for Palestinian rights. That could be a key difference between Rahul Gandhi and his parents. What makes it curious is that he can be vividly stern with the right-wing establishment at home while favouring a gentler diplomatic vocabulary for outrages abroad.

Rahul’s father, like him, also lived in tricky times. A standoff with China in Arunachal Pradesh melted into a handshake with Deng Xiaoping. Ties with Pakistan soured over India’s military exercise close to its border, which Islamabad saw as a cover for invasion. Rajiv Gandhi dissolved the tensions by holding a memorable meeting with Benazir Bhutto in Karachi. Relations soured with the king of Nepal, Sinhala nationalists as well as Sri Lanka’s Tamil separatists. Rajiv helped create Saarc in 1985 to thrash out the differences. Modi has shut that option.

Finally, a Pakistani student at the US event asked Rahul how ties could improve between the neighbours. Rahul could have recalled how he and Priyanka watched a cricket match in Karachi in 2004 while the two countries discussed terrorism. Why aren’t those India-Pakistan matches played any longer? Rahul could have said that the IRA was furiously attacking British targets, keeping the Good Friday Agreement on the anvil for years. His idea of ‘talks only after terrorism ends’ sounds like Modi’s foreign ministry statement. Or, perhaps it shows the right wing in Congress is alive and kicking, perhaps to Sonia Gandhi’s chagrin.

The writer is Dawn’s correspondent in Delhi.

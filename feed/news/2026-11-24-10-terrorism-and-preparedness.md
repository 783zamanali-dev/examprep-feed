> Why it matters: This article directly addresses critical issues of terrorism, internal security, governance challenges, and resource management in Pakistan.



The November 11 armed attack on a police contingent at Darbukach in Kohat's Gumbat tehsil portrays a disturbing picture of the erosion of the writ of the state in Khyber-Pakhtunkhwa. Reports emanating from different sources indicate that ten police personnel were martyred while returning from an operation against illegal placer-gold mining. Police also reported killing eighteen assailants. This loss follows attacks on the Speen Mela Police Post at Hassan Khel, Bannu's Gumbati checkpost, and a Karak ambush that claimed the lives of four policemen. Praising the valour and sense of duty of the fallen is necessary to keep motivating the force engaged against terrorism, yet it remains insufficient. Their sacrifice makes it all the more urgent to examine why such violence persists. Gold deposits along the Indus, from Kund and Khairabad towards Shakardarra, should have been a gift of God for local communities and a boost to the provincial economy. Unfortunately, that belt has been swarmed by armed gangs busy in illegal extraction, reportedly with the support of militant networks. Areas rich in natural resources appear to have been turned into a proverbial "Wild West", where these groups impose their will at gunpoint. The police themselves allege a nexus between illicit miners and militants in Kohat. Given such lethal retaliation, enforcing mining laws now extends far beyond ordinary mineral regulation. The incident also brings the issue of operational preparedness to the fore. Was adequate intelligence obtained about the adversaries and their capacity? Was the surrounding terrain, including possible vantage points, properly assessed? Were withdrawal routes, communications, reinforcements and casualty evacuation planned for a hostile environment? Was it anticipated that, in such an eventuality, the force might encounter a more sophisticated militant threat? Yet without an independent, professional inquiry, these possibilities cannot be treated as established facts. The objective of posing these questions is to improve training, equipment, command arrangements and officer safety - not to diminish the courage of those who died. Another dangerous aspect is the shadowy economy it sustains. Violent non-state actors can gain access to mining, mint money, control transport, influence mineral markets and nurture relationships with people whose livelihoods have become dependent upon illegal activity. Some residents may view them as providers of income or dispute resolution; others may submit out of fear. Both circumstances weaken intelligence gathering, encourage recruitment and sustain militant finances. The government must, therefore, go after not just the gunmen, but also the financiers, buyers, suppliers, facilitators and anyone else who protects these illegal operations. The reported task given to the Area Representative and Development Council to engage with militants raises another serious concern. At a press conference on November 11, a spokesperson for the Council, quoting the Prime Minister, said that militancy could vanish from the twelve disturbed districts forever within three months if council members and residents persuaded militants to leave. But what does "leave" mean? Would militants lay down their weapons, renounce violence and live in accordance with the law? Or would they simply move to greener pastures, cross the border or regroup? Shifting them elsewhere cannot eliminate the threat, nor does it constitute a lasting restoration of state authority. My own experience serving in conflict zones in senior police and administrative positions, reinforced by research for my PhD thesis, "Fault Lines of Governance: Challenges of Militancy and Extremism in Pakistan", suggests that comparable measures have not produced the desired results in the past. Tribal elders may be effective in mediating disputes of a local nature, but they cannot be expected to neutralise organised armed movements. Indeed, the South Asia Terrorism Portal recorded at least 194 tribal elders killed in 143 incidents between 2005 and early March 2024. Many were targeted for standing with the government. To expose their successors to similar risks without enforceable protection is to ignore this tragic history. The 2022 negotiations - followed by the TTP ceasefire - which involved tribal elders, failed to yield the dividends of peace. Demands regarding the withdrawal of security forces and the reversal of FATA's merger became major obstacles. Dialogue and peace agreements are not inherently objectionable, but their terms and conditions must be unambiguous, and in no case should they appear to be a surrender of the writ of the state. The mandate, safeguards, verification mechanisms and consequences for non-compliance must be clearly defined. Political scientist Francis Fukuyama's concept of "stateness first" is relevant here. Following 9/11, he emphasised that weak institutions result in disorder and the proliferation of transnational security threats. Yet stateness means the capacity to enforce legitimate laws and deliver basic public goods, not arbitrary coercion. Governance ultimately concerns citizens' security, justice, welfare and ability to live without fear. Ideological extremism, including the selective misuse of religious narratives to legitimise violence, must be challenged; however, security operations alone cannot fill the institutional vacuums in which armed groups thrive. The response must combine intelligence-led policing, a capable Counter-Terrorism Department, sound investigations and credible prosecutions with decisive action against illicit mining and militant financing. Transparent licensing, environmental safeguards and legitimate employment can help detach communities from the underground economy. In the merged districts, meaningful administrative and judicial integration must be matched by adequate financial resources. The announced special funding for police capacity and anti-drone systems should translate into measurable protection on the ground. Furthermore, the government should publish verifiable data on attacks, extortion, kidnappings, illegal mining, prosecutions and civilian safety. The true measure of the writ of the state lies not in temporary clearance operations, casualty counts or promises that militants will depart within three months. Its real test is whether citizens can travel, work, attend school and obtain justice without seeking permission from armed groups. In essence, the duty to ensure the security and freedom of citizens belongs solely to the state; it cannot be delegated to vulnerable elders.

> Why it matters: This is a critical resource security issue directly impacting Pakistan's agriculture-based economy.



PUNJAB’S looming water crisis should serve as a wake-up call for both federal and provincial policymakers. A new assessment by the UN Food and Agriculture Organisation warns that water demand in the province already exceeds supply across all sectors. Even more alarming is the annual depletion of 5.5bn cubic metres of groundwater. The province needs an additional 10bn cubic metres of usable water every year to bridge the supply-demand gap and sustain its aquifer. This shows the consequences of years of neglect and an approach to water management that has placed little emphasis on conservation. The FAO findings are based on water accounts covering 2012-17 but the trends hold. Punjab’s agricultural economy, expanding cities and growing population are placing increasing pressure on a resource that is already under severe stress.

Agriculture, which accounts for the bulk of freshwater use, must be central to any serious conservation strategy. Much of Punjab still relies on traditional flood irrigation, an inefficient method that results in substantial losses. There is an urgent need to encourage a transition towards water-efficient technologies. This can help farmers produce more with less water. Crop choices also need to reflect local water availability. Furthermore, there is a case for developing smaller, distributed water storage facilities along riverbeds and suitable locations in the riverine belt. Such reservoirs, including off-channel storage and recharge ponds where feasible, could capture surplus flows during periods of high availability for use when supplies fall. This would offer a complementary and potentially cheaper option to large dams, whose construction involves substantial financial, environmental and social costs. Much of the rain that cities receive is channelled into drainage and sewerage systems due to rapid construction and the spread of concrete. Better urban planning must make rainwater harvesting, permeable surfaces, recharge wells and retention ponds integral to new housing schemes and public infrastructure. Cities need investment in systems that capture and store rainwater. Wastewater treatment and reuse, too, should become part of the province’s water strategy. The FAO’s proposal for a provincial water commission similarly deserves serious consideration.
